## Thesis

A training run spends three budgets: arithmetic, memory and time between failures. The arithmetic is about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token. For Llama 3 405B, 6ND is 3.80e25 FLOPs, Meta's stated budget, and at the run's own measured throughput it accounts for 80% to 90% of the 30.84M GPU-hours Meta published (T01-5 to T01-7; 01 W7). The memory is 16 bytes per parameter for mixed-precision Adam before a single activation is stored, so even the 8B model's 128.5 GB of model state is 1.6 times one 80 GB H100 (T01-9; 01 W2).

Parallelism splits those two budgets across accelerators, and each split pays with a collective whose bytes and frequency decide which link it can ride. Tensor parallelism fires inside every layer of every micro-batch and stays on NVLink, 450 GB/s per GPU per direction on a p5.48xlarge; data and pipeline parallelism fire once per step or per micro-batch and cross EFA at 50 GB/s per GPU [derived] (T02-13, T02-14, T02-46, T02-68, T02-70). That one trade turns "set my ranks" into a procedure: shard model state across data-parallel ranks, add tensor parallelism up to the instance, add the smallest pipeline that fits across instances, nest the fastest dimension innermost, and measure the two or three layouts that pass (02 section 3.5). For Llama 3.1 70B on 256 H100 at 8,192-token sequences and a 4.2M-token batch, two layouts pass the checks: TP 8 x PP 4 x DP 8 with ZeRO-1 and five interleaved chunks per stage, a bubble of about 0.9% at micro-batch 1 [derived], and TP 8 x FSDP 32 with each rank's 16 sequences as one micro-batch and full recomputation, the layout TorchTitan published for this model on 96 GB H100s with Float8, at a sequence length its benchmark does not state (T02-50; 02 section 3.2; W2-44).

Time between failures is the third budget. A synchronous job stops when any node stops, so its failure interval divides by the node count: Llama 3 405B, trained on up to 16K GPUs, saw an unexpected interruption every 3.1 hours over a 54-day window and still kept more than 90% effective training time (T04-1, T04-4, T04-7). Checkpoint interval, asynchronous saves and restart time are how a run spends that budget; at 256 GPUs failures cost about 0.5% to 4% of wallclock at Meta's measured RSC-1 failure rate across the save designs modelled, so the design changes the interval more than the goodput, while at 16K GPUs restart time decides the outcome [derived, our reading, 04 W3].

On AWS the step is the same on every path, and what changes is who owns the cluster around it. SageMaker training jobs run a cluster per job at 1.15 times the EC2 rate; HyperPod runs a long-lived Slurm or Kubernetes cluster with burn-in checks, node replacement and resume at 1.20 times on P5 and P6; AWS PCS runs only the Slurm controller, for a fee; self-managed EC2 and EKS leave the rest to the team (T05-23, T06-4 to T06-7, T06-36, T06-43). On every path the training script owns its checkpoints, the reservation's end, and the per-step log a hang detector reads (section 20).

### Layer A. Framing and the reader's map

**1. A training run is three budgets**
Depth: survey
Payoff: Estimate a run's arithmetic, memory and failure budgets before you choose a framework or an AWS service.

Opens on the outcome a team needs before a run: how many accelerators for how long, how to lay the model across them, which software and which AWS path, and how to keep the run alive and know it is healthy. Names the dive's six questions in order (how much compute and how long; will it fit, and how do I split it; which software; how do I keep it running; which AWS path and what it costs; how do I know it is healthy) and leaves the map of sections to section 2. Then the thesis in plain words, which is the text the page starts from; every figure comes from the thesis and is defined later in its own section: "Training a large language model spends three budgets. Arithmetic: about 6 floating-point operations for every parameter, for every token the model trains on. Memory: about 16 bytes for every parameter before the model has read a single token, so even an 8-billion-parameter model needs about 128 GB, more than one 80 GB H100 holds. Time between failures: a job spread over thousands of GPUs stops whenever any one of them fails, which on Meta's Llama 3 run, on up to 16,000 GPUs, happened about every three hours. Splitting the work across GPUs pays for the first two budgets in network traffic, and the split that works keeps its most frequent traffic inside one instance, where GPUs exchange data about nine times faster than between instances. On AWS the training step is the same on every service; what differs is who runs the cluster, who replaces a broken machine and who restarts the job." Mental model: every technique trades one resource for another among arithmetic, memory, communication and time (recomputation, re-running part of the forward pass instead of storing it, trades arithmetic for memory; sharding, splitting the model state across GPUs instead of copying it, trades communication for memory; asynchronous checkpoints, saves written in the background, trade host memory for time lost to failures). ### Layer B. One accelerator: the step, its arithmetic, its memory

**3. What one training step computes, and what it keeps**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Name every byte and every FLOP of a step before you split any of them.

Introduces the step as two passes and one update: the forward pass, the backward pass (which computes two gradients, one for each layer's input and one for its weights), and the optimizer update. The forward pass costs about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token, the same arithmetic an inference engine spends on a prompt, and the backward pass about 4, so a step costs about 6N FLOPs per token (T01-14). In numbers: by the 6N rule, one 8,192-token sequence of Llama 3.1 8B costs about 3.95e14 FLOPs before attention, which section 4 adds (13% to 27% more at this length), about one second of one H100 at the 40% efficiency section 4 explains, and the model state behind it is 128.5 GB, with another 36.5 GB of activations for that one sequence (FlashAttention kernel, fused SwiGLU, no recomputation; derived in section 5 and measured per layer in E1), against the 80 GB of one H100 in a p5.48xlarge [derived, 02 section 3.3, 01 W2, W4]. The update then reads the gradients and the optimizer state and writes new weights. Two kinds of state follow, and the whole dive turns on the difference: model state (weights, gradients, optimizer state) persists from step to step and scales with parameters; activations live only from a micro-batch's forward pass to its backward pass and scale with tokens in flight (T01-10, T01-11). Defines micro-batch, gradient accumulation and global batch with one identity, B = b x m x d (micro-batch x accumulation steps x data-parallel size), because every later sizing decision uses it and Calculator C solves it (T01-52). The notation block every later formula uses sits here and is repeated in section 32: N parameters (all parameters for memory and for the FLOPs figures the dive quotes; section 4 notes the matmul-only count); D training tokens; s sequence length in tokens; b micro-batch size in sequences; m micro-batches per step per data-parallel rank; d data-parallel degree; B = b x m x d, the global batch in sequences (B x s tokens); L layers; h hidden size; a attention heads; V vocabulary size; t tensor-parallel degree; p pipeline stages; v interleaved chunks per stage; n ranks in one collective; r_f failures per node-day; w_cp the time a checkpoint save blocks training. Every traffic formula states whether it counts elements or bytes. Mixed precision is introduced here only as "compute in 16 bits, keep an FP32 master copy for the update", with the reason: an update more than about 2,048 times smaller than its FP16 weight vanishes when added (T01-39, T01-41); section 6 develops formats. Mental model: a step is a fixed bill in FLOPs and a fixed bill in bytes, and distributed training is the art of splitting both. Practical value, and the ending: the two bills of the reader's own model, in FLOPs per token and bytes per parameter, before any split. Diagram: one step as a timeline (forward, backward, optimizer) with the two kinds of state drawn as bars: model state constant across the step, activations rising through the forward pass and falling through the backward pass.

**4. Counting the arithmetic: 6ND, MFU and how long a run takes**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Estimate a run's accelerator-hours from three numbers, and know what the estimate leaves out.

Opens with the efficiency measure the whole dive uses. MFU (model FLOPs utilization) is the share of the accelerators' dense peak that the model's own arithmetic achieves: tokens per second x FLOPs per token, divided by accelerators x dense peak. It counts only the model's FLOPs (6N plus attention), so work the hardware repeats, such as recomputation (re-running a layer's forward pass during the backward pass instead of storing its activations; section 5), raises HFU (hardware FLOPs utilization) and leaves MFU unchanged. The training-time identity follows: time = 6ND / (accelerators x dense peak x MFU) (T01-58; Megatron-LM's variant with recomputation sits in the evidence disclosure, T01-23). Used once before it is dissected: Llama 3.1 70B on 1T tokens is about 297,000 H100-hours at 40% MFU, 48 days on 32 p5.48xlarge [derived, 01 W8]. Three corrections make it honest. Attention adds 12Lhs FLOPs per token on top of 6N, so the rule undercounts as sequences grow: for Llama 3.1 8B attention is 12.5% (causal kernel) to 22% (no causal discount) of per-token FLOPs at 8,192 tokens and passes half at about 57K (causal) or 29K tokens [derived, 01 W6] (T01-17, T01-18, C01-2). Strictly, the N in 6N is the matmul parameter count, which excludes the embedding table: using the full count overstates the 8B model's FLOPs by 7% [derived, 01 W1]; Meta's 3.8e25 budget matches 6ND with either count (3.80e25 full, 3.78e25 matmul-only), and the tokens-per-second figures in section 29 use the full count; Calculator A states which it uses, with the other as a toggle. Recomputation's extra FLOPs are why Megatron-LM's "52% of peak" and Llama 3's 38% to 43% MFU are different quantities (T01-19, T01-21, T01-22, C01-5). MFU divides by the dense peak at the training precision (the matrix-unit peak without 2:4 structured sparsity); NVIDIA's pages print the sparse figure, twice the dense one, so an H100's BF16 (bfloat16: 16 bits with FP32's exponent range) denominator is 989.5 TFLOPS (T01-27, T01-29). The check against a real run: 6ND for Llama 3 405B is 3.80e25 FLOPs, Meta's stated budget, and Llama 3 405B's published 30.84M GPU-hours are 1.1 to 1.3 times the 6ND estimate at the run's own measured 380 to 430 TFLOPs per GPU [derived, 01 W7] (T01-5, T01-6, T01-7). The same model card's 8B and 70B hours are about 2.9 and 1.6 times a 40%-MFU estimate at 15T tokens (the card says "15T+", so the ratios are upper bounds), and the card does not say what its hours include (U01-3), so the 405B ratio is one anchored data point, not an overhead rule. The section's strongest number, landed twice: 6ND reproduces Meta's own 3.8e25-FLOP budget for Llama 3 405B. Published MFU for context goes in an expandable block: PaLM 540B 46.2% with attention FLOPs counted, 45.7% without (T01-20), MegaScale 55.2% for a 175B model on 12,288 Ampere GPUs at 2,048 tokens, against Megatron-LM's 41.2% at the same 6,144-sequence batch, where in its 256-GPU ablation model changes (parallel transformer block and sliding-window attention) supplied 5.6 of the 17.6 points gained and a LAMB-enabled tripled batch 3.0 (T01-24, T01-25), Llama 3 405B 38% to 43% (T01-6); DeepSeek-V3 reports GPU-hours but no MFU (T01-26). Scaling-law intuition enters only as far as it sets D: Chinchilla-optimal is about 20 tokens per parameter (T01-56), and production models are over-trained on purpose because they are served many times (Llama 3.1 8B saw about 1,870 tokens per parameter, T01-57). Practical value, and the ending: **Calculator A, run time and accelerator-hours**, with model, tokens, sequence length (with the causal or no-discount attention choice, 01 W6), instance (figures from section 21's table), accelerator count and an MFU slider (default 40%, inside Llama 3's measured range, labelled as an assumption; precision fixed at BF16 for the MFU default, since Llama 3's measured range is BF16; NVIDIA's own FP8 benchmarks for Llama 3 put achieved model FLOPs at about 35% to 42% of the dense FP8 peak on H100 and B200 [vendor claim, derived], so an FP8 option, if added, defaults below 40% of the FP8 peak, U01-5), showing the 01 W8 table as presets and a cost line that reads section 22's dated rates. Diagram: per-token FLOPs as a stacked bar (dense 6N, attention) across sequence lengths 2K to 128K for 8B and 70B.

**5. The memory budget: 16 bytes per parameter, then activations**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Know whether a model fits, and which lever buys back memory at what price.

Model state first. Mixed-precision Adam holds 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for BF16 weights, 2 for BF16 gradients, and 12 for the optimizer's FP32 master weights and its two moments (running averages of each gradient and of its square) (T01-9, T02-5). Megatron keeps FP32 main gradients, 18 bytes per parameter (T03-17), so every byte total in the dive states its gradient dtype. Llama 3.1 8B therefore needs 128.5 GB of model state before a single activation, 1.6 times an 80 GB H100; 70B needs 1,128.9 GB at 16 bytes and about 1,270 GB at 18 [derived, 01 W2, 03 W4]. The optimizer owns the memory, and alternative optimizers change the 12: DeepSeek-V3's BF16 moments with FP32 master and gradients (14 bytes total), 8-bit Adam (10), Muon (12), Adafactor without momentum (about 8), each row assuming BF16 weights and gradients plus an FP32 master copy that the training framework keeps, not the optimizer; Megatron Core's opt-in precision-aware optimizer can hold that master as a 16-bit remainder beside the BF16 weight (2 bytes instead of 4) and the moments in BF16 or FP8 (T01-47, T01-59, T01-60, T01-61; code read at the tags in wave 2, wave2-step-maths G U01-12). Activations second. Per layer they are about 34 sbh bytes for Llama's layer with a FlashAttention kernel, our derivation from Korthikanti et al.'s method (T01-31, T01-38; 01 W3, U01-11); with eager attention the 5as/h term makes them about ten times larger at 8,192 tokens (and the gap grows with sequence length). The coefficient also depends on the SwiGLU implementation: E1 measured 34.0 sbh per layer with a fused SwiGLU and 41.0 sbh with the eager `F.silu(gate) * up` that TorchTitan v0.4.0's model code computes (Tier 0, one GB10, PyTorch 2.15.0); full recomputation stored exactly 2 sbh. Llama 3.1 8B at 8,192 tokens and micro-batch 1 holds 36.5 GB of activations across 32 layers, plus 4.2 GB of FP32 logits from its 128,256-entry vocabulary when the loss upcasts them, as Meta's reference code and Megatron Core's default loss do (a fused cross-entropy such as Transformer Engine's keeps a 2.1 GB BF16 copy instead), which a GPT-era rule of thumb calls negligible and which here is 11.5% of the layer total [derived, 01 W4] (T01-35, C01-1, C01-3); E1 measured the saved 4.27 GB, and a transient peak of 8.5 GB in the forward pass and 11.6 GB in the backward pass around the head and loss (Tier 0, one GB10). The levers, each with its price: sharding model state over data-parallel ranks (section 8), tensor plus sequence parallelism (section 9, divides activations by t), full recomputation (stores 2sbh per layer for about 33% more FLOPs; E1 measured exactly 2sbh, and E2 measured a 14% to 16% longer step for a 1B Llama-shaped model at 2,048 tokens on one GB10, below the 25% to 40% the papers report on their models), selective recomputation (single-digit percent in Megatron's attention-only form), and a smaller micro-batch (T01-12, T01-32 to T01-34, T01-36, T01-37; E1, E2 Tier 0). Pipeline parallelism is the lever that does not cut peak activations: the first stage holds L layers' worth whatever p is (T01-62, T02-19). Beyond the formula sit temporary buffers and fragmentation (T01-11, T01-63): TorchTitan's measured 70B peak for TP 8 x FSDP 32, on 96 GB H100s with Float8, local batch 16 and full recomputation, was 67.6 to 71.9 GiB (72.6 to 77.2 GB), against about 39 GB from the formula for the same layout, batch and recomputation in BF16 at 8,192 tokens [derived, 02 section 3.2]; the precision differs and the run's sequence length is not confirmed (U02-5), so read the gap as a reminder that buffers and fragmentation add real memory, not as a calibration (T02-50, U02-8). Practical value, and the ending: **Calculator B, the memory budget**, with model, optimizer row, gradient dtype (the 16- and 18-byte rows), sharding stage and degree, TP with or without SP, pipeline degree with interleaved chunks, context-parallel degree, recomputation mode, micro-batch and sequence length, returning model state, activations, logits and the headroom left on the chosen accelerator (or on one logical NeuronCore for Trainium), with a headroom target as a fraction of HBM, default 75% (60 GB of an 80 GB H100), past which a result is flagged "confirm with a measured step" [our reading; no source states a headroom figure]. **6. Precision for training: BF16, FP8 and the microscaled formats**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Pick a training precision your accelerator runs in its matrix units, and know what each step down costs.

Introduces the idea the section needs first: a narrow format covers a small range of magnitudes, so a tensor is stored as format values times a shared scale factor, the same idea as inference quantization scales (the silicon dive's "Quantization and Precision"). Loss scaling is the FP16 case: one multiplier on the loss keeps small gradients above FP16's smallest value. Then the mental model: lower precision is a story about where the scale factor lives. FP16 needs one loss scale shared by every tensor in the model, a constant in the original recipe, which also proposed adjusting it on overflow (T01-40); BF16 has FP32's range and needs none (T01-41); FP8 needs a scale per tensor, E4M3 for weights and activations and E5M2 for gradients in the original proposal (T01-42, T01-44); DeepSeek-V3 used E4M3 for every FP8 matmul input, with a scale per 1x128 tile of activations and per 128x128 block of weights, and kept embedding, output head, attention and normalisation in BF16 or FP32 (T01-46, T01-47, C01-7); MXFP8 shares a power-of-two scale across 32 values (T01-48); NVFP4 uses an FP8 scale per 16 values plus one per tensor (T01-49). Each step down moves the scale closer to the data. What it costs, from first-party runs: in its proposers' runs, which simulated FP8 by rounding matmul inputs to FP8 values and computing in 16 bits, FP8 matched 16-bit training within run-to-run noise up to GPT 175B, the 175B comparison against a BF16 baseline taken at 75% of training (T01-43); DeepSeek-V3 reports a relative loss error under 0.25% against BF16 on validation runs of about 1T tokens on two smaller models (T01-47); NVIDIA's NVFP4 run on a 12B hybrid model tracked FP8 within 1% relative loss, widening past 1.5% during learning-rate decay, with 16% of linear layers kept in BF16 (T01-50). What each accelerator runs: Transformer Engine (NVIDIA's library that runs transformer layers in FP8 and FP4) needs compute capability 8.9 for FP8, 9.0 with CUDA 12.9 or later for FP8 block scaling, and Blackwell (10.0) for MXFP8 and NVFP4, and picks MXFP8 by default on Blackwell (T01-45); FP8 on H100 therefore runs through Transformer Engine in Megatron Core, Accelerate or NeMo AutoModel, since TorchTitan removed its Float8 path in v0.4.0 and now offers MXFP8 and experimental NVFP4, on Blackwell only (T03-13, T03-19, T03-41, STALE); Trainium2's Tensor Engine takes cFP8, BF16, FP16, TF32 and FP32 with hardware stochastic rounding (T01-51), yet the PyTorch-native Neuron stack trains Trn2 in BF16 only, with FP8 and MXFP8 gated on Trainium3 (T03-36, T03-53, C03-10). The FP32 master copy survives every format (T01-39, T01-47, T01-48), which is why precision changes the compute bill far more than the memory bill. In numbers: an H100's dense FP8 peak is 1,979 TFLOPS against 989.5 for BF16 (T05-14, a vendor specification), so the 70B preset of 297,000 H100-hours at 40% MFU falls to about 148,500 at the same MFU against the FP8 peak, an upper bound on the gain, while the model state stays at 1,128.9 GB [derived, 01 W2, 01 W8]. Practical value, and the ending: the choice per accelerator, BF16 on Trn2, FP8 through Transformer Engine in Megatron Core on Hopper, MXFP8 on Blackwell, with the per-accelerator table of formats and recipe names in a disclosure. ### Layer C. Many accelerators: splitting the step

**7. Collectives: five operations and what each one costs**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Price any parallelism in bytes per link before you run it.

Introduces the vocabulary every later section uses, starting with who talks. A rank is one training process; on GPUs one rank drives one GPU, and the world size is the number of ranks in the job. Each parallelism has a degree, the number of ranks it splits across, and the degrees multiply to the world size: TP 8 x PP 4 x DP 8 is 256 ranks, or 32 p5.48xlarge. Each rank therefore holds a coordinate on a grid, which PyTorch calls a device mesh (section 13). Megatron Core expects consecutive ranks to share an instance (T02-51), and the two common launchers number ranks that way: torchrun gives each node a contiguous block of ranks, and DeepSpeed's launcher numbers ranks node by node in hostfile order; TorchTitan and DeepSpeed then lay their meshes out row-major with TP last, so the grid's innermost dimension is the one that stays on NVLink (W2-3). Four codebases supply most of the formulas, defaults and published layouts from here on: PyTorch's own distributed APIs (DDP and FSDP2) with TorchTitan, the PyTorch team's trainer built on them; NVIDIA's Megatron Core, with Megatron Bridge as its front end; and DeepSpeed. Section 15 maps them as engines and the front ends that configure them. Then the operations. All-reduce, reduce-scatter, all-gather, all-to-all and point-to-point send and receive, each defined by what every rank holds before and after. The identity that explains half of the dive: an all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather, so a bandwidth-optimal all-reduce moves 2(n-1)/n of the data per rank, and reduce-scatter, all-gather and all-to-all each move (n-1)/n (T02-4, T02-60, T02-61). Algorithm bandwidth against bus bandwidth, as nccl-tests reports them, so the reader can read a benchmark (T02-60). Ring against tree: the same bandwidth, latency linear against logarithmic in rank count (T02-62). The bandwidth hierarchy on p5.48xlarge, per GPU per direction: 450 GB/s on NVLink against 50 GB/s on EFA, a factor of 9 [derived, 02 section 3.4] (T02-68, T02-70); DeepSeek's H800 cluster had a ratio of 3.2 (T02-39). The time estimate the procedure in section 14 uses: bytes over per-GPU link bandwidth, compared against compute time; achieved bus bandwidth sits below these link peaks (U02-9). In numbers: all-reducing Llama 3.1 8B's BF16 gradients (16.06 GB) moves 2 x 7/8 x 16.06 = 28.1 GB per GPU inside one p5.48xlarge, about 62 ms at 450 GB/s; across four instances a flat ring moves 2 x 31/32 x 16.06 = 31.1 GB per GPU, bounded by 50 GB/s of EFA per GPU, about 0.62 s, ten times longer for the same gradients [derived, 01 W2]. Configuration of NCCL on EFA belongs to the EFA dive's "NCCL over EFA: the aws-ofi-nccl Plugin", which this section links; the inference form of the same model is in the vLLM dive's "Distributed Inference" and the SGLang dive's "Parallelism: TP, DP Attention & EP". Mental model: every parallelism trades a replicated or full-size object for a collective, and the collective's bytes times its frequency decide which link it can live on. Practical value, and the ending: price a layout's collectives in bytes per link before running it, and later read a measured nccl-tests bus bandwidth against these peaks (section 31). Diagram: the five collectives as before-and-after rank grids, with the reduce-scatter plus all-gather decomposition of all-reduce drawn explicitly.

**8. Data parallelism and sharding: DDP, ZeRO, FSDP and HSDP**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Shard model state across data-parallel ranks, and keep the sharding on the fast link.

Data parallelism first: every rank holds a full copy of the model (a replica) and averages gradients with one all-reduce per step, which PyTorch DDP overlaps with the backward pass by launching it bucket by bucket, 25 MiB per bucket by default at PyTorch v2.15.0 with a smaller first bucket (`bucket_cap_mb`; T02-1 to T02-3, W2-1). Its traffic per step is 2N(n-1)/n gradient elements per rank, close to 2N at any useful rank count, so it barely grows with the number of ranks (T02-4, T02-60). ZeRO then removes the redundancy in three stages: optimizer state (4 + 12/d bytes per parameter per rank), plus gradients (2 + 14/d), plus parameters (16/d) (T02-6, 01 W2). Stages 1 and 2 cost no extra bytes, because an all-reduce already was a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather; stage 3 adds one all-gather, 3N elements per step, 1.5 times DP (T02-7). FSDP (Fully Sharded Data Parallel; FSDP2 is PyTorch's current version, the `fully_shard` API) generalises stage 3 with a sharding factor F between 1 (DDP) and the world size, and hybrid sharding (HSDP) maps each sharding group to one instance, so all-gathers and reduce-scatters stay on NVLink and only an all-reduce of each GPU's 1/G shard crosses EFA, 2M(W-G)/(GW) bytes per GPU for an M-byte gradient across W GPUs in sharding groups of G (T02-8 to T02-10; the paper prints F = W/G where its own definition gives F = G, and a looser 2M(W-1)/(GW); C02-1, W2-14). ZeRO++ attacks the same cross-node bytes with quantised gathers and a secondary in-node copy (T02-11). The worked example carries the section: Llama 3.1 8B on four p5.48xlarge with eight accumulation micro-steps. At FSDP2's defaults (gradients synced on every micro-batch), flat FSDP over 32 GPUs moves about 373 GB per GPU per step across EFA and HSDP with one instance per sharding group about 24 GB, a factor of about 15; with sync deferred to the last micro-batch on both, about 156 GB against about 3 GB, a factor of about 50 [derived, 02 section 3.3, 03 W1; the 156 GB all-gather count, k + 1 per FSDP unit for k micro-batches, measured in E3 at PyTorch 2.15.0, U03-1 closed]. The defaults behind those numbers go in an expandable block attached to the HSDP recommendation: FSDP2 reduce-scatters and re-gathers on every accumulation micro-batch unless the trainer turns sync off, TorchTitan keeps that default, from PyTorch 2.15 an unset `reduce_dtype` reduces FP32 gradients in FP32, which raises the flat-FSDP step to about 498 GB, and deferral costs a full unsharded gradient per rank (T03-2 to T03-4, C03-9) [derived, 03 W1]. Data parallelism has a ceiling that is not a link: past the critical batch size (beyond which a larger batch stops cutting the number of steps in proportion; T01-53) more replicas waste compute, and at a fixed global batch more DP ranks shrink each rank's batch, which Llama 3 names as the reason its MFU fell from 43% to 41% going from 8,192 to 16,384 GPUs (T01-53 to T01-55). Mental model: cross the slow link once, fan out on the fast one. Practical value, and the ending: shard optimizer state first, keep the sharding group inside the instance, set the gradient-sync mode deliberately, and treat the global batch as the cap on DP. Diagram: DDP, ZeRO-1, ZeRO-3 and HSDP drawn on two instances of eight GPUs, with each collective coloured by the link it rides.

**9. Tensor and sequence parallelism: splitting every layer**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Split each layer across the GPUs of one instance, and see why that is where tensor parallelism stops.

Tensor parallelism is the split inference engines use when a model does not fit one GPU: each GPU holds a slice of every weight matrix and computes a slice of every layer. Training adds the backward pass, so each block needs a collective in both directions, and adds sequence parallelism for what TP leaves replicated. Megatron's column-then-row split lets the MLP's nonlinearity run locally and needs one all-reduce per block in the forward pass and one in the backward; attention splits by heads (T02-12). Its traffic scales with activations, not parameters: 8bsh(t-1)/t elements per layer per micro-batch (T02-13). For Llama 3.1 70B at TP 8 on one p5.48xlarge and 8,192 tokens that is 0.94 GB per layer per micro-batch of one sequence, 2.1 ms on NVLink against 13.3 ms of layer compute at the 40% MFU working assumption, about 16% if none of it overlaps [derived, 02 section 3.2]. The frequency is the reason for Megatron's first takeaway, TP up to the GPUs of one server and no further (T02-14). The divisibility rules a framework enforces: heads divide by t, and KV heads (query groups under grouped-query attention) are a multiple or divisor of t (T02-15). Sequence parallelism splits the layer norms and dropout that TP leaves replicated along the sequence and swaps each all-reduce for a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather: the same bytes, so it costs nothing in volume, though the paper measures the pair as slower than one all-reduce; its value is the activation memory it divides by t (T02-16 to T02-18, C02-8). Mental model: section 7's bytes-times-frequency rule, applied; TP fires inside every layer of every micro-batch, which pins it to the fastest link. Practical value, and the ending: TP no larger than the GPUs in one instance (8 on p5), always paired with SP, and checked against the head counts. Diagram: one transformer layer split across four GPUs, Megatron's f and g operators marked (f is identity forward and all-reduce backward; g is the reverse), and the per-layer bytes beside the NVLink and EFA bandwidth bars.

**10. Pipeline parallelism and the bubble**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Size the bubble before you add stages, and know which memory pipelining does not save.

Stages hold consecutive layers; micro-batches flow through them; the idle time at the start and end of each step is the bubble. Stages need not hold equal layer counts: Llama 3 405B moved one layer off its first and last stages to balance embedding and loss work and ran 126 layers on PP 16 as 7 + 14 x 8 + 7 [derived, R3 review; T02-35], and DeepSeek-V3 ran 61 layers on PP 16 under DualPipe, which puts the shallowest and deepest layers on the same rank; the paper does not give the per-stage counts (T02-40, T02-71, W2-26). Megatron Core requires layers to divide by p only when no uneven-stage option is set; `--decoder-first-pipeline-num-layers`, `--decoder-last-pipeline-num-layers`, the two `--account-for-*-in-pipeline-split` flags and `--pipeline-model-parallel-layout` lift it. TorchTitan v0.4.0 counts the embedding and the output head as one layer each by default and splits the rest evenly, so its default already gives the first and last stages one layer fewer; DeepSpeed's pipeline balances stages by parameter count by default (W2-5). GPipe's bubble over total time is (p-1)/(m+p-1) (T02-27); over ideal time it is (p-1)/m (T02-28), and the two denominators make quoted percentages incomparable unless stated (C02-9). 1F1B (one forward, one backward: once the pipeline is full, each stage alternates one micro-batch's forward with another's backward) keeps the same bubble and caps in-flight micro-batches at p (T02-29); interleaving v chunks per device divides the bubble by v at v times the pipeline traffic and needs m to be a multiple of p (T02-30). Later schedules go in an expandable block: Zero Bubble splits the backward into input and weight gradients to fill the gaps (T02-33); DeepSeek's DualPipe runs micro-batches from both ends and overlaps compute with all-to-all (T02-34); Llama 3 decoupled micro-batch count from stage count and moved a layer off the first and last stages to balance embedding and loss work (T02-35). Pipeline traffic is small: bsh/t per micro-batch per boundary, 16.8 MB for the 70B example, 0.34 ms on EFA against 266 ms of stage compute, which is why PP is the dimension that crosses instances (T02-31, 02 section 3.2). The memory fact a newcomer gets wrong: under 1F1B the first stage holds L layers' worth of activations whatever p is, so PP divides weights and optimizer state, not peak activations (T02-19, T01-62). Megatron called activation recomputation "required" with pipelines in 2021; Llama 3 trained 405B with PP 16 at 8K without it (T02-32, STALE, C02-11). Worked bubble: 70B on 256 H100 at TP 8 x PP 4 x DP 8, micro-batch 1, gives m = 64 and a 1F1B bubble of 4.7%, and interleaving with v = 5 brings it to 0.94% [derived, 02 section 3.2]. Mental model: the bubble is (p-1)/(v x m), and the global batch caps m. Practical value, and the ending: the smallest p that fits, then raise m or v until the bubble is near one percent. Diagram: an interactive schedule chart (GPipe, 1F1B, interleaved) for p = 4 and a slider for m, with the bubble shaded.

**11. Long sequences: context parallelism**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Split one long sequence across GPUs when its activations no longer fit, and pick ring, all-gather or all-to-all by your attention layout.

Context parallelism splits every activation along the sequence, unlike sequence parallelism, which splits only the norms and dropout (T02-25). Attention needs every token's keys and values, so CP pays for its split with communication of K and V. Three published designs: Ring Attention passes K and V blocks around a ring and hides the transfer behind block compute when the block of c tokens is long enough, c >= per-GPU peak FLOPS / per-GPU link bandwidth in bytes per second (T02-20, T02-21); DeepSpeed-Ulysses uses two all-to-alls so each GPU holds the full sequence for a subset of heads; under grouped-query attention the split follows the key-value heads, so DeepSpeed v0.19.9 rejects a Ulysses degree above the KV-head count (8 for every Llama 3.1 size) and splits a count that does not divide evenly unevenly, without replicating KV heads (T02-22, T02-23, W2-2); Llama 3 all-gathers K and V and accepts the exposed transfer because grouped-query attention makes K and V small, with a two-chunks-per-rank layout that balances the causal mask (T02-24). For Llama 3.1 70B (64 heads, 8 KV heads) on a p5.48xlarge, the ring's overlap condition gives about 2.2K tokens per block over NVLink and about 19.8K over EFA before grouped-query attention's 8x reduction of K and V [derived, 02 section 3.4]. CP replicates weights, so its ranks fold into the data-parallel gradient reduction and it saves no parameter memory (T02-26). Llama 3's 128K stage used CP 16 at 38% MFU (T02-47). Mental model: long sequences hide their own communication, because attention compute grows with s squared while CP traffic grows with s. Practical value, and the ending: add CP when the activations left after TP with SP, and after the recomputation you accept, do not fit Calculator B's budget, or at the lengths where Llama 3 used it (CP 16 at 131K tokens); Megatron Core recommends CP from 8K tokens (T02-25, T02-42), while Llama 3 trained 405B at 8K with CP 1 (T02-47) and both 70B layouts in section 14 use none; then choose the variant by head layout and mask. Diagram: the three variants side by side on four GPUs, with the bytes each moves per attention layer.

**12. Mixture of experts: expert parallelism**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Place experts so the all-to-all stays inside the fast domain, and size the expert grid separately from the dense one.

Introduces the mixture-of-experts layer in two sentences (a router sends each token to k of E experts) and links the silicon dive's "Mixture of Experts and Sparse Activation" for why sparsity exists and for the dispatch mechanics and the keep-it-in-the-fast-domain rule as inference sees them; this section keeps what training adds. Expert parallelism places experts on different GPUs and moves tokens to them with an all-to-all dispatch and an all-to-all combine per MoE layer, forward and backward; the bytes are k copies of each token's hidden state each way (T02-37, T02-38). The capacity factor sets how many tokens each expert accepts per batch, as a multiple of an even share, and tokens past it are dropped (T02-36). DeepSeek-V3's answer to a slow cross-node link: route each token to at most 4 nodes, cross InfiniBand once per node, fan out on NVLink, and run 64-way EP across 8 nodes with PP 16 and no TP on 2,048 H800s (T02-39, T02-40), the mental model of section 8 (cross the slow link once, fan out on the fast one) applied to tokens; EP 64 would fit inside one P6e-GB200 UltraServer's 72-GPU NVLink domain and span 8 p5.48xlarge over EFA [derived from section 21's domain sizes]; per token per MoE layer that is about 172 KB of forward dispatch-plus-combine before de-duplication [derived, 02 section 3.4]. Megatron Core's MoE parallel folding gives attention and expert layers separate grids that share only PP, and its own guide's "Total GPUs = TP x PP x CP x EP x DP" disagrees with the code, which factorises the two grids separately (T02-41, T02-72 WRONG, C02-2). Mental model: an MoE model runs two parallel layouts in one model. Practical value, and the ending: keep EP x expert-TP inside the NVLink domain, prefer EP over TP for fine-grained experts, check the world size against both grids (T02-42), and treat the result as candidate grids to test: the registers hold no activation formula for MoE or multi-head latent attention layers, so Calculator C sizes MoE model state and expert-parallel bytes only and labels activation memory "not modelled; measure a step". Diagram: dispatch and combine for one token across two instances, with node-limited routing drawn as one InfiniBand or EFA hop and an NVLink fan-out.

**13. Composing the dimensions: how frontier runs are laid out**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Read any published training layout as which link each dimension rides and why its size was chosen.

Introduces the device mesh as the object every layout is: the ranks of section 7 arranged as a grid with one axis per parallelism, whose degrees multiply to the world size, and a rank order that decides which axis lands inside an instance. The published layouts, read on that grid with the previous six sections. Llama 3 405B: TP 8 x CP 1 x PP 16 x DP 64 on 8,192 GPUs at 43% MFU (TP 8 is one p5.48xlarge's NVLink domain, and 8,192 GPUs would be 1,024 p5.48xlarge [derived]), DP 128 on 16,384 at 41%, CP 16 at 131K tokens at 38%, nested [TP, CP, PP, DP] from innermost to outermost because the innermost needs the most bandwidth and least latency (T02-46, T02-47). DeepSeek-V3: no TP, PP 16, EP 64, ZeRO-1 DP (T02-40). TorchTitan: FSDP2, TP, PP and CP composed on one device mesh, with FSDP alone often enough up to about 512 GPUs when communication is faster than compute (T02-49, T02-50; its paper and its repository benchmarks disagree on numbers, C02-3). Megatron Core: default rank order "tp-cp-ep-dp-pp", with adjacent ranks expected to share a node (T02-51, C02-4). The evidence for adding model parallelism at that scale: on A100s in 2021, Megatron's PTD-P (pipeline, tensor and data parallelism combined) layout beat ZeRO-3 without model parallelism (TP and PP, the dimensions that split the model itself) by 6% and 24% for 175B and 530B at the smaller GPU counts, and by 70% after the GPU count doubled at a fixed global batch, which the authors attribute to less cross-node communication (T02-43 to T02-45). Mental model: nest innermost-fastest. Every source puts TP innermost, inside one instance, with CP next to it; the outer two differ: Llama 3 nests [TP, CP, PP, DP] with DP outermost, while Megatron Core's default order (tp-cp-ep-dp-pp), TorchTitan's device mesh and DeepSpeed's pipeline topology put PP outermost and DP inside it. Once TP fills the instance, PP and DP both cross EFA under either order (T02-46, T02-51, W2-4). Practical value, and the ending: write your layout as a mesh with its innermost axis inside one instance, and check that your launcher's rank order puts consecutive ranks on one instance before you size anything. Diagram: the 4D mesh as nested boxes, Llama 3's degrees filled in, each level labelled with its collective and link.

**14. Set my ranks: the sizing procedure and calculator**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Turn a model, a cluster, a memory budget and a global batch into two or three layouts worth measuring.

The dive's operational centre. A 12-step procedure assembled from the published ones (02 section 3.5; [our reading] where no single source states a step): (1) gather inputs: model shapes, instance, sequence length, global batch and a memory headroom target; (2) check 16N bytes (18N with FP32 gradients) against HBM; (3) shard model state over DP before adding model parallelism: Megatron's distributed optimizer (its ZeRO-1) or ZeRO-1 first, then ZeRO-2, then FSDP, and HSDP when cross-instance bytes dominate; (4) add TP up to the instance size, with SP; (5) add the smallest PP that fits; uneven first and last stages are allowed (Megatron Core's `--decoder-first-pipeline-num-layers` and `--decoder-last-pipeline-num-layers` or its `--account-for-*` flags, TorchTitan's default split, DeepSpeed's parameter-balanced partition; section 10); (6) add CP only when activations still do not fit (section 11); (7) set the expert grid separately for MoE; (8) check divisibility: the degrees multiply to the world size (TP x CP x PP x DP for the dense layers, expert-TP x EP x expert-DP x PP for the expert layers), and each degree divides what it splits (heads by TP, layers by PP with the uneven-stage exception, the global batch by micro-batch x DP); (9) check the bubble; (10) check bytes per link against compute; (11) nest innermost-fastest (section 13); (12) measure the survivors for a few hundred steps (T02-5, T02-6, T02-9, T02-14, T02-15, T02-19, T02-25, T02-30, T02-35, T02-41, T02-46, T02-52, T02-54, T02-57, T02-58). What the published tools do and where they disagree: NeMo 2.x's auto-configurator searched TP, PP, CP, EP and micro-batch and measured (T02-54, C02-6), and was not located in any current NeMo-family repository, the NVIDIA/NeMo repository having become speech-only at v3.0.0 (T03-25, T03-27, U03-2); DeepSpeed's autotuner tunes the ZeRO stage, the micro-batch size and other ZeRO settings, and takes the model-parallel degree as a fixed input (T02-56); Hugging Face's Ultra-Scale Playbook gives fit-then-batch-then-optimise steps (T02-57, Tier 3); and for Llama 3.1 8B on one node NeMo starts at TP 2 or 4, TorchTitan at FSDP 8 and NVIDIA's guide at CP 2 (T02-55). Two worked examples carry the section, one where the first choices work and one where they fail. Llama 3.1 70B on 32 p5.48xlarge at 8,192 tokens and a 4.2M-token batch: TP 8 x PP 4 x DP 8 with ZeRO-1 and five interleaved chunks (about 38 GB per GPU on paper with BF16 gradients, about 43 GB with Megatron's FP32 main gradients, bubble about 0.9%, DP traffic about 1% of a 17.5 s step) and TP 8 x FSDP 32 (TorchTitan's published layout, run with each rank's 16 sequences as one micro-batch and full recomputation; FSDP traffic then about 6% of the step, against 94% if the same layout ran 16 micro-batches at FSDP2's default per-micro-batch sync; measured at 67.6 to 71.9 GiB on 96 GB parts with Float8) both survive [derived, 02 section 3.2] (T02-50, W2-44). Megatron Bridge's recipe for the same model on 32 H100, TP 4 x PP 4 x VP 5 x CP 2 (VP 5 is five interleaved chunks per stage, section 10's v), holds 52.9 GB of model state per GPU with the distributed optimizer and 79.4 GB without it, which would leave under 1 GB for activations [derived, 03 W2]; Megatron Core's guide recommends the distributed optimizer for all multi-GPU training anyway (T03-24, T03-17, T02-52). The second example, where the first choice fails: Llama 3.1 70B on four p5en.48xlarge (32 H200 of 141 GB; NVIDIA's figure, AWS lists 141 GiB). ZeRO-1 and ZeRO-2 over 32 GPUs fail the memory check at 308.7 and 172.0 GB per GPU; flat FSDP 32 fits only with full recomputation and then spends about 70% of compute time on EFA at FSDP2's defaults; HSDP inside one instance holds 141.1 GB of model state per GPU, the whole of an H200, and fails once any activation is stored; TP 8 x DP 4 with ZeRO-1 fits at about 85 GB per GPU with no pipeline and no recomputation (about 103 GB with FP32 gradients), with TP 8 x PP 2 x DP 2 at about 67 GB as the margin option [derived, R3 review; wave 2 verifies]. On 32 H100 the same TP 8 x DP 4 layout needs the same 85 GB without recomputation and does not fit; it fits on paper at about 64 GB only with full recomputation, a third more compute, so the H200's memory removes either the pipeline or the recomputation, which is section 21's point in one layout [derived, W2-55]. The 8B case is section 8's worked example. Practical value, and the ending: **Calculator C, parallelism sizing**, which takes a model (presets for Llama 3.1 8B, 70B and 405B, DeepSeek-V3 in the MoE mode below, or custom shapes), an instance type and count (with ranks per instance and HBM per rank, so a trn2.48xlarge is 64 ranks of 24 GiB at LNC=2, T03-38, 03 W3), a sequence length, a global batch and a memory headroom target. It enumerates TP, PP, interleaved chunks, CP, DP or FSDP degree, micro-batch, recomputation mode and FSDP's gradient-sync mode; checks each candidate's memory through Calculator B, deferral's unsharded gradient included; and ranks the survivors by one estimated step time (compute from section 4, times the recomputation factor from section 5, times one plus the bubble from section 10, plus exposed communication from sections 7 to 9 as a range between full overlap and none), showing the per-link shares behind each figure. Layouts that need uneven first and last stages pass with the flag "uneven stages: Megatron Core needs `--decoder-first/last-pipeline-num-layers` or `--pipeline-model-parallel-layout`; TorchTitan's default already splits this way" (W2-56). For MoE models it reports model state per GPU on both grids, expert-parallel bytes with node-limited routing, and the world-size check, labels activation memory "not modelled; measure a step", and words its output as candidate grids to test. It ends on "measure these two or three", which is section 31's layout bake-off. An agent can call the same function, specified once for both. ### Layer D. The software that implements it

**15. The framework map: engines, front ends and maturity labels**
Depth: survey
Payoff: Choose a training framework by the engine underneath it, at a pinned version, after reading its maturity label.

Opens with the mental model that organises the field [our reading, assembled from T03-16, T03-23, T03-26, T03-42, T03-50]: a handful of engines implement parallelism (PyTorch's FSDP2 and DTensor, its distributed tensor that is one logical tensor sharded or replicated across a device mesh; Megatron Core; DeepSpeed), and everything else is a front end that picks a configuration and a loop (TorchTitan, Megatron Bridge, NeMo AutoModel, Accelerate, the transformers Trainer, and every post-training framework), so choosing a framework is mostly choosing an engine (T03-16, T03-23, T03-26, T03-42, T03-50). One idea carries four names: ZeRO's stages are DeepSpeed's `stage`, Megatron-FSDP's `optim`, `optim_grads` and `optim_grads_params`, Megatron's distributed optimizer, and PyTorch's `fully_shard` (T03-1, T03-17, T03-18, T03-29). The comparison table of register 03 section 3 is the section's spine, one row per framework at its pinned tag: PyTorch v2.15.0, TorchTitan v0.4.0, TorchTitan Neuron 0.2.2+neuron.1, Megatron Core core_v0.20.0, Megatron Bridge v0.7.0, NeMo AutoModel v0.7.0, DeepSpeed v0.19.9, Accelerate v1.15.0, transformers v5.22.1, with columns for parallelism and pipeline schedules, training precision, checkpoint format and resharding, configuration style and launcher, the project's own maturity words, AWS integration, and best fit [our reading]. The facts this section owns, each from code at the tag: the PyTorch-native stack rests on APIs its own docs call alpha (DTensor, pipelining), experimental (tensor parallel, `async_save`) and prototype (`context_parallel`) (T03-6); resharding (loading a checkpoint saved under one layout or rank count into another) differs by engine: Megatron's default optimizer checkpoint reshards only across data-parallel ranks unless saved fully reshardable, while PyTorch Distributed Checkpoint (DCP) reshards at load and DeepSpeed through an offline conversion of an ordinary checkpoint, so on Megatron a change of TP, PP or EP (not a data-parallel-only change of node count) is decided before the checkpoint is written; a `dp_reshardable` checkpoint loaded under a new TP or PP fails with an error that points to a deprecated flag (T03-9, T03-22, T03-32, C03-3, wave2-frameworks U03-3); DeepSpeed's ZeRO is off unless configured, and its configuration has no FP8 switch: FP8 comes from Transformer Engine layers in the model, which DeepSpeed's own tests run under ZeRO stages 0 to 3 (T03-29, T03-33; W3-13); Accelerate defaults to FSDP1 while the transformers Trainer defaults to FSDP2 for the same plugin (T03-40, C03-6); the NVIDIA/NeMo repository became speech-only at v3.0.0, with LLM training moved to Megatron Bridge, AutoModel and NeMo RL (T03-25, T03-26, STALE); torchtune is no longer actively maintained (T03-48). A one-line index points to the defaults other sections own: TorchTitan's removed Float8 (section 6), FSDP2's per-micro-batch sync (section 8), Megatron's distributed optimizer off by default (section 14, T03-17), Megatron's `--async-save` condition (section 18). Nanotron enters only as the code behind the Ultra-Scale Playbook (U03-9). Practical value, and the ending: a three-question chooser (which accelerator and precision, which model source, how much of the stack you want to read) that points at an engine and a front end, with the version to pin. **16. Fine-tuning and post-training runs as systems**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Size a fine-tuning or RL run's memory and pick the engine it should reuse.

Fine-tuning reuses everything above with one change to the memory budget: freezing the base weights removes their gradients and optimizer state. LoRA (low-rank adaptation: small trainable matrices beside frozen weights) stores optimizer state only for the adapters, up to two thirds less training memory for a large model in its authors' words; QLoRA (LoRA over a base quantised to 4 bits) also quantises the frozen base to 4-bit NormalFloat and fine-tuned a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU (T03-43, T03-44). For Llama 3.1 70B before activations: full fine-tuning about 1,129 GB of model state at 16 bytes per parameter, or about 1,270 GB at Megatron's 18 bytes with FP32 main gradients: 15 and 16 H100 at a conservative 80 GB each, 14 and 15 at the 80 GiB the EC2 page lists; LoRA about 141 GB, two H100; 4-bit QLoRA about 35 GB, one [derived, 01 W2, 03 W4; T03-17]; LLaMA-Factory's own estimated table prints 1,200, 160 and 48 GB (T03-45). The configuration-file frameworks (Axolotl v0.21.1, LLaMA-Factory v0.9.5) run these on FSDP2, DeepSpeed or Megatron Core (T03-46, T03-47). Reinforcement learning (RL) and preference training are inference jobs inside training jobs; the RL dive owns the algorithms and the step-as-a-system view (its section 19), and this section adds only what a systems reader needs: every post-training framework read in this research reuses a training engine (verl on FSDP, Megatron or TorchTitan; OpenRLHF on DeepSpeed; TRL on Accelerate; slime on Megatron; NeMo RL on DTensor or Megatron Bridge), so its parallelism, checkpoint and precision behaviour is the engine's [our reading] (T03-49, T03-50; RL dive register 05). TorchTitan's RL stack moved out of its experimental folder in v0.4.0 (T03-15). On AWS, the HyperPod recipes are mostly fine-tuning and post-training recipes (section 26). Practical value, and the ending: pick LoRA, QLoRA or full fine-tuning from Calculator B with the frozen-base row selected, then pick the framework by the engine you already run. ### Layer E. Keeping a run alive

**17. Failure is the steady state: interruption rates and goodput**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Predict how often your job will stop from its node count, before you design anything to survive it.

Opens with the mental model: a synchronous job is a series system. It stops when any node stops, so its mean time to failure is the node's divided by the node count, MTTF = 1 / (N_nodes x r_f) (T04-7). Meta measured r_f at 2.34 and 6.50 failures per thousand node-days on its two research clusters, with a bad month near 17.5, and job MTTF falling from 47.7 days for 8-GPU jobs to 7.9 hours for 1,024-GPU jobs (T04-7, T04-8). The same rates give the reference 256-GPU job (32 p5.48xlarge) a hardware interruption every 2 to 13 days and a 16,384-GPU job one every 0.7 to 5 hours [derived, 04 W1]; an order-of-magnitude planning aid, because the paper's counting rules differ between its two measures. The first-party reports agree on the shape. Llama 3 405B, trained on up to 16K GPUs, saw 466 interruptions in 54 days, 419 unexpected, one every 3.1 hours over the 54 days, about 78% confirmed or suspected hardware, and still kept more than 90% effective training time (T04-1, T04-2, T04-4; Table 5's first row is internally inconsistent, so the page quotes counts, C04-1, U04-1). The other operator reports go in an expandable block, one anchor figure each. OPT-175B's notes cover about 90 restarts over the final model's lineage; at the 27% mark the team put most of the 40-plus restarts since mid-November down to hardware failures, and restarts stalled when checkpoint downloads from the blob store hung (T04-17, T04-18). MegaScale restarted over 100 times in several weeks on more than 10,000 GPUs and recovered over 90% of faults automatically (T04-13). Meta's research clusters: removing 40 "lemon" nodes (24 on RSC-1, 1.2% of that cluster, and 16 on RSC-2, 1.7%) cut the failure rate of 512-GPU-plus jobs from 14% to 4% (T04-12), and hardware failures touched under 1% of jobs but 19% of GPU runtime (T04-11). Effective training time ratio (ETTR, productive time over wallclock) is the goodput measure the dive uses, and about 90% is what the best operators report at 10K to 16K GPUs with heavy automation (Llama 3, MegaScale; T04-1, T04-13); Meta's research clusters estimate 0.85 to 0.9 for their largest 2K to 4K GPU runs, assuming Daly-Young intervals and 5-minute restart and save costs (T04-11). AWS's HyperPod pages state "60-80% typical goodput on self-managed clusters" and over 95% with checkpointless training (recovery from peers' in-memory state instead of a saved checkpoint; section 25); these are vendor claims without stated conditions and are shown labelled, apart from the operator reports (T04-52, C04-5). The section's strongest number, landed twice: one unexpected interruption every 3.1 hours over a 54-day window of Llama 3 405B pre-training, on up to 16K GPUs. Practical value, and the ending: read your node count across the W1 table to get the interruption interval your checkpoint and restart design must beat, and screen nodes before they join (section 24's deep health checks; Meta's lemon-node lesson). Diagram: MTTF against node count on log axes, one line per measured r_f, with the 256-GPU and 16K-GPU points marked.

**18. Checkpointing: what to save, how often, how fast, and where**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Pick a checkpoint interval and a storage size from your node count and your save time.

What a checkpoint holds: weights and optimizer state (most of the bytes), plus the data-loader position, RNG states, scheduler and step counter, which are kilobytes to megabytes and decide whether a resumed run is the same run (T04-23, T04-35). Gradients are not saved, so a mixed-precision Adam checkpoint is about 12 to 14 bytes per parameter (14 with BF16 weights saved beside the FP32 master copy, 12 when the master is the only weight copy) [our reading, from T01-9's components]: about 988 GB for Llama 3.1 70B at 14, 3.86 GB per GPU on 256 GPUs [derived, 04 W2]; cross-checks from real runs: OPT-175B wrote 1.6 TB in 992 files, Llama 3 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU (T04-5, T04-18). How often: the Young and Daly interval, with w_cp the time a save blocks training,, dt* = sqrt(2 w_cp / (N_nodes r_f)), balances the time a save blocks against the work lost per failure (T04-9; the primary papers sit behind publisher access and the formula is cited through Meta's study, T04-10, U04-2). For the 70B job on 256 GPUs at RSC-1's 6.50 failures per thousand node-days and a 10-minute restart: 2.0 hours for a 60 s blocking save, 21.5 minutes for an asynchronous save (one that copies state off the GPU and writes it in the background) assumed to block about 2 s, with expected ETTR 0.982 against 0.995; at 16K GPUs the same arithmetic gives 0.79 for a 60 s save against 0.88 for a 2 s save at the same 10-minute restart, and 0.93 only if the restart also falls to 5 minutes, so restart time dominates; at that scale the job's 1.8-hour MTTF is below the regime in which Meta checked the approximation against a Monte Carlo estimate (job MTTF of about 0.1 day or more, accurate to about 5%), and the interval is shorter than the restart, so the values are indicative [derived, 04 W3; T04-9]. How fast: for a synchronous save the aggregate write bandwidth is bytes over blocking time, 16.5 GB/s for a 60 s save of the 70B checkpoint; for an asynchronous save it is bytes over a drain budget no longer than the interval, because the storage drains host memory while training continues [our reading]. On FSx for Lustre (AWS's managed parallel file system; section 23) bandwidth is bought with capacity: about 16 to 17 TiB at PERSISTENT-1000 (the 1,000 MBps-per-TiB tier), provisioned in 2,400 GiB steps, on the order of $10,000 to $12,000 a month at $0.600 per GB-month (T00-1), because the per-client ceilings section 23 lists are far above the per-node need (T04-29, T04-30, T05-35, T05-36) [derived, 04 W2, 05 section 3.5]. On S3, the request rate per prefix sets the floor and prefix strategies exist for it (T04-26, T04-27, U04-8); S3 Express One Zone's per-bucket rates change the picture (T04-28). Asynchronous and tiered designs: PyTorch DCP saves in parallel from every rank and reshards at load (T04-19); `async_save` called bare stages synchronously into pageable memory, so the fast path needs an explicit pinned-memory stager, which TorchTitan's `async_with_pinned_mem` mode sets (T04-20 to T04-22, C04-2); Megatron turns `--async-save` off, with only a rank-0 warning, for distributed checkpoints unless `--use-persistent-ckpt-worker` is also set (T04-38, C04-4); MegaScale copies each GPU's state to pinned host memory in several seconds and writes it to HDFS in the background (T04-14); Gemini (an in-memory checkpointing system from Rice University and AWS, SOSP 2023) also copies it into a peer machine's memory, so most failures recover from RAM, with remote storage as the backstop (T04-33, T04-34); HyperPod managed tiered checkpointing (EKS, generally available since 2025-09-08) offers host-memory tiers as a managed feature. AWS's library for it is a DCP storage plugin with no process-group code, and its published example creates an NCCL-only process group and calls `async_save` with no stager; PyTorch v2.15.0 rejects that group ("A CPU backend must be enabled for async save", reproduced in E4 on the DGX Spark), and with a CPU backend added the bare call blocks for the whole device-to-host copy (E4). Initialise with "cpu:gloo,cuda:nccl" and pass a pinned `DefaultStager` (T04-31, T04-32, C04-3, U04-4 closed, E4). Mental model: save is asynchronous, load is not. Practical value, and the ending: **Calculator D, checkpoint interval and storage**, which takes checkpoint bytes (from Calculator B's optimizer row), node count, a failure-rate preset (2.34, 6.50, 17.5 per thousand node-days), step time, save mode, blocking time and restart time, and returns the interval in steps, expected ETTR with a validity warning when 1 / (N_nodes r_f) falls below 0.1 day, and the storage target (bytes over blocking time for a synchronous save, bytes over the drain budget for an asynchronous one) with the FSx capacity or S3 layout it implies. **19. Resuming correctly: restart, elasticity, stragglers and determinism**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Make a restart resume the same run, and find the slow rank before it costs you a tenth of the cluster.

Resuming correctly means the restarted job continues the same run: the same weights and optimizer state, the same position in the data, the same random streams. The launcher's restart, elasticity, determinism and stragglers decide whether it does. Restart mechanics, from the launcher up: `torchrun` restarts every worker when any one fails, up to `--max-restarts`, which defaults to 0, and ranks are not stable across restarts (T04-40); Slurm's `--requeue` reruns the batch script under the same job ID and `--signal` warns a job before its end (T04-41); HyperPod adds node replacement and auto-resume around both (section 24 and 25, T06-6, T06-7, T06-9). Elastic training changes only the data-parallel dimension, because tensor and pipeline layouts are baked into each rank's shard: HyperPod elastic training resizes the data-parallel dimension by checkpointing and resuming at the new size, and a per-scale configuration can hold the global batch (AWS's example keeps 128 at every scale, with uneven local batches where it does not divide); TorchFT (PyTorch's fault-tolerance library) adds or drops replica groups between steps without a restart, a recovering replica copies live state from a healthy peer instead of from storage, and in its default mode the gradient is averaged over the replicas present, so the global batch shrinks while a replica is missing unless spare replicas are configured (T04-42, T06-13, T06-14). Determinism: data order should be a function, not a history; Megatron derives document, sample and shuffle indices from a seed and checkpoints only a counter, and `torchdata`'s `StatefulDataLoader` checkpoints batches yielded (T04-44, T04-45; MosaicML Streaming's elastic determinism is a vendor claim, T04-46). Bitwise reproducibility is not guaranteed across releases or platforms and costs speed; TorchTitan's determinism switch sets the knobs (deterministic algorithms, cuDNN deterministic mode with benchmarking off, a fixed cuBLAS workspace) and logs a warning to expect slower training (T04-24, T04-43). Neither PyTorch's reproducibility notes nor NCCL's user guide (2.32.3) says whether a collective returns bitwise-identical results run to run; NCCL picks its algorithm from the topology unless `NCCL_ALGO` pins it, so a different node set can change the reduction order [our reading] (U04-5, accept-as-hedge; experiment A1 repeats one all-reduce on a fixed topology). Stragglers cost as much as failures and hide better: a ByteDance trace of 3,079 jobs found 42.5% straggling, 10.4% of GPU-hours lost, problems on individual workers (hardware or software on one machine) explained more than half of the slowdown in only 1.7% of straggling jobs; the most common causes the authors found were in the workload and the training code: unevenly partitioned pipeline stages, unbalanced sequence lengths across micro-batches, and Python garbage-collection pauses (T04-47, T04-48). The other straggler studies go in an expandable block: MegaScale gained 0.7% MFU by removing hosts about 10% slower (T04-15); Llama 3 found stragglers by ranking suspect collectives (T04-6); Alibaba's fail-slow episodes ran from tens of seconds to nearly ten hours (T04-49). Mental model: the waiting shows up in collectives, so a straggler is found by per-rank timing of compute, not by the rank that is waiting. Practical value, and the ending: set `--max-restarts` above 0 or use the orchestrator's resume, checkpoint the data-loader state, derive data order from a seed, and log per-rank step time so section 29's straggler check has its input. Diagram: one step across eight ranks with one slow rank, showing the other seven waiting inside the all-reduce.

**23. Storage for training on AWS: data in, checkpoints out**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Size training storage for the checkpoint burst and the restart read, because the tokens barely register.

The arithmetic that sets the design first: at 40% MFU and 4 bytes per token (Megatron's on-disk format for a 128K vocabulary), the 70B job on 256 H100 reads about 1 MB/s of tokens in total; Meta's 405B run, at its reported 400 TFLOPs per GPU on 16,384 GPUs, would read about 11 MB/s in the same format, from a storage fabric built for 2 TB/s sustained [derived, 04 W5] (T04-5, T04-50). **24. The Slurm family: your own Slurm, AWS PCS and HyperPod Slurm**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Pick the Slurm path by who runs the controller and who checks the GPUs.

Slurm is the scheduler most large training teams already know, and AWS offers it three ways. Self-managed on EC2, with AWS ParallelCluster as the deployment tool: still maintained at v3.16.2 and supported to 2028-02-20, while every page of its user guide now opens with a banner recommending PCS (T06-38). AWS PCS (AWS Parallel Computing Service): AWS runs and patches the Slurm controller, the customer brings an AMI carrying the PCS agent (a PCS-ready Deep Learning AMI, DLAMI, exists for GPU), cluster size is fixed at creation (Small up to 32 instances, Medium 512, Large 2,048), SchedMD ships a Slurm major every six months and PCS supports each for about 18 months from SchedMD's release, so a PCS cluster takes a Slurm major upgrade about once a year to stay supported, and after end of life a cluster can run up to 12 more months without guaranteed support (T06-32), and GPU health checks and requeue remain the customer's (T06-31 to T06-35, T06-37). HyperPod Slurm: AWS provisions controller, login and worker groups on its HyperPod DLAMI, shaped by lifecycle scripts or, since 2026-07-13, by a custom AMI built on HyperPod's base AMI, runs a health-monitoring agent on every node, offers deep health checks when nodes join (NVIDIA Data Center GPU Manager (DCGM) diagnostics level 4 at about 45 to 90 minutes), reboots or replaces faulty nodes by default, always replaces them for a job step run with auto-resume, and resumes a job step launched with `srun --auto-resume=1` from its last checkpoint (T06-3 to T06-7; the HyperPod Slurm overview ties replacement-and-resume to clusters of more than 16 nodes, while the auto-resume page and the Slurm FAQ state no such limit, C06-8, U06-1). Fees on 32 p5.48xlarge for a 30-day month: PCS Small $15,165 beyond EC2 [derived, 06 section 3.2] (T06-36: $0.5825 per controller-hour and $0.64 per P or Trn instance-hour, us-east-1, 2026-10-30); HyperPod's premium sits in the ml.* instance rate (T06-18; size from section 22). EC2 topology-aware Slurm placement is the EFA dive's ("The EC2 Instance Topology API"). Practical value, and the ending: within the family, a team that wants AWS to own node health runs HyperPod Slurm with deep health checks on and `--auto-resume=1` on every training step; one that wants its own images and only a managed controller runs PCS, budgets the fee and the yearly upgrade, and adds its own GPU health checks and requeue; ParallelCluster stays for clusters already running. **25. The Kubernetes family: self-managed EKS, Batch on EKS and HyperPod EKS**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Assemble the Kubernetes pieces a training job needs, and know which ones AWS can own.

What a Kubernetes cluster needs before it can run synchronous training: accelerated nodes (Auto Mode, self-managed Karpenter, or node groups; Capacity Blocks through a launch template targeting the reservation, T06-41, T06-42), device plugins (NVIDIA, EFA, and the Neuron dynamic resource allocation (DRA) driver or device plugin, T06-45; EFA enablement is the EFA dive's "EFA on Amazon EKS"), a gang scheduler (Kueue with all-or-nothing admission and topology-aware scheduling, or Volcano, T06-44, T06-47), and a job API. Self-managed EKS now repairs GPU nodes itself: the node monitoring agent reads Xid errors (the NVIDIA driver's numbered GPU error reports; section 30) from DCGM and automatic node repair replaces or reboots the node (T06-43); AWS's EKS best-practice guide for AI/ML compute tells training clusters to turn this on and to stop Karpenter consolidating busy training nodes (`consolidationPolicy: WhenEmpty`) (U06-9). AWS Batch on EKS is an overlay that creates its own labelled nodes, scales them to zero when idle, and gang-schedules multi-node parallel jobs (T06-49, T06-50). HyperPod EKS keeps the customer's control plane and owns the worker nodes: health agents whose findings become labels and taints, deep health checks, node recovery, task governance on Kueue, and a training operator that restarts failed processes in place and detects hangs from log patterns, with elastic training (data-parallel replicas grow and shrink, resuming through PyTorch DCP) and checkpointless training (recovery from peer state, NeMo-based, from 2025-12-03; it keeps at least two full replicas of the distributed optimizer state, `num_distributed_optimizer_instances` of 2 or more, which is the HBM it spends to recover from peers) on operator v1.2 or later (T06-2, T06-8, T06-9, T06-12 to T06-16; the operator's support matrix lags the platform, C06-2). ### Layer G. Operating a run

**29. What to measure in a training run**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Watch three layers of signal, and know which layer moved when a run goes wrong.

Opens with the mental model: a run reports on three layers, each with its own tools. Model health is loss, gradient norm and NaN or Inf; job efficiency is tokens per second per accelerator, MFU and the shares of step time; hardware health is Xid errors, ECC and row remapping, throttling, NVLink and EFA counters. A drop in efficiency with healthy loss points at infrastructure; a loss spike with steady throughput points at the model, the data, or a silent hardware fault (T07-1, T07-20, T07-21 to T07-26). TorchTitan's default log is the reference list: global average and maximum loss, gradient norm, tokens per second per device, achieved TFLOPS, MFU, and the data-loading share of step time (T07-1, T07-2; its logged gradient norm is the pre-clip total, C07-3). MFU becomes something a reader checks in tokens: at 40% MFU, counting 6N FLOPs per token with the full parameter count and no attention term, an H100 trains Llama 3.1 8B at about 8,200 tokens per second and 70B at about 935, so 8B at 4,000 tokens per second is near 20% MFU [derived, 07 W1]. TorchTitan's MFU column also counts attention and leaves out the embedding table, so at 8,192-token sequences its 40% is about 6,800 tokens per second for 8B and about 820 for 70B, and 4,000 tokens per second reads as about 23% [derived from the v0.4.0 FLOP count]. Gradient norm is the leading indicator and loss the lagging one: OLMo 2 found gradient-norm spikes more frequent than loss spikes and often ahead of them, with a slow upward drift preceding divergence, and defines a spike as 7 standard deviations over a 1,000-step rolling window (T07-18); OPT-175B's team watched gradient norm and loss scale and intervened by lowering the learning rate and restarting (T07-14, T07-15); PaLM 540B saw about 20 loss spikes despite clipping and restarted about 100 steps earlier, skipping 200 to 500 batches (T07-13); Llama 3 405B and DeepSeek-V3 report stable runs with no divergence interventions or rollbacks (T07-16, T07-17). TorchTitan asserts finite loss and gradient norm before every optimizer step, and Megatron checks for NaN by default (T07-19, T07-20). Memory: peak allocated, peak reserved and fragmentation from the allocator, and the memory snapshot for the allocation that pushed the peak (T07-3, T07-4); the EKS Best Practices Guide's example adds allocated to reserved, which double counts (T07-30, WRONG). Efficiency at the device: "GPU utilization" only says a kernel ran during the sample. For streaming multiprocessor (SM) activity, NVIDIA calls 0.8 or more necessary but not sufficient for effective use and says below 0.5 likely indicates ineffective use; AWS's EKS guide sets 0.8 as a rule-of-thumb target (T07-23, T07-29, T07-40); DCGM v4.7.0 renamed these fields and keeps the old names as aliases (T07-22). The data-loading share of step time, with 20% as the level the EKS guide sets for optimising the pipeline (T07-41); per-rank step time for stragglers (section 19). Practical value, and the ending: the measurement table (metric, how it is computed, the healthy range only where a source gives one, the source) and the alert set that follows from it: a sustained throughput drop, a gradient-norm spike score, any NaN or Inf, SM activity below 0.5, data loading above 20% of step time, and any Xid whose catalog bucket is RESET_GPU, RESTART_BM or CONTACT_SUPPORT [our reading]. Diagram: the three-layer signal stack, with common symptoms mapped to the layer that explains them.

**30. The telemetry stack on AWS**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Turn on the signals that are off by default, on whichever path you run.

The telemetry stack is the set of exporters, stores and dashboards that carry section 29's signals off the nodes; on every path several of them are off until you turn them on. Hangs first, because a synchronous job that hangs costs the whole cluster. At PyTorch v2.15.0 a collective on the NCCL process group times out after 10 minutes and the process crashes rather than continuing; a heartbeat monitor watches the watchdog itself: its threshold is 8 minutes (`TORCH_NCCL_HEARTBEAT_TIMEOUT_SEC=480`), and because it compares the watchdog's heartbeat at checks 8 minutes apart it declares a stall 8 to 16 minutes after it begins, then dumps and waits up to another 8 minutes before killing the process [derived from the v2.15.0 code]; before teardown every rank dumps a flight-recorder trace of its last 2,000 collectives (about 2.5 steps of FSDP collectives for the 8B example at eight micro-batches and about one step for 70B, from E3's per-unit counts [derived]), on by default since v2.11.0 without configuration, and `torchfrtrace` matches them across ranks and names the rank that never joined (T07-5 to T07-11; the PyTorch tutorial's stated defaults are stale against the code, T07-12, C07-1). From the stuck collective to teardown takes about 11 minutes at defaults, about 6% of the mean time between Llama 3's interruptions, which is the case for leaving the timeout alone without a reason [derived, 07 W2]. NCCL's reliability, availability and serviceability (RAS) subsystem runs in every process from NCCL 2.24 and answers which peer is missing (T07-27). On the GPU side: DCGM profiling fields and dcgm-exporter 4.8.4, whose default set leaves out SM activity and the throttling and ECC counters (T07-21, T07-24); NVIDIA's Xid catalog with its resolution buckets (T07-25); clock-event reasons for throttling (T07-26). EFA counters are the EFA dive's ("Operations, Observability and Failure Modes"); Neuron monitoring and profiling are the silicon dive's. The AWS layer, path by path, every fact dated 2026-10-30: CloudWatch Container Insights with enhanced observability collects NVIDIA, EFA and Neuron metrics on EKS once its add-on is installed (T07-28); the SageMaker HyperPod observability add-on publishes to Amazon Managed Service for Prometheus and Amazon Managed Grafana, with training KPIs, EFA and Neuron metrics off by default; four of its preset GPU alerts (ECC double-bit errors, power violation, thermal violation, NVLink errors) read DCGM fields that AWS's metrics reference lists as advanced metrics, off by default, so those rules have no data until advanced DCGM metrics are turned on [our reading of AWS's metrics and alerts pages and the dcgm-exporter CSV they name; not tested on a cluster] (T07-31, T07-32, C07-8, U07-3); SageMaker training jobs publish per-instance metrics in which GPU utilisation is summed across GPUs, 0 to 800% on a p5 (T07-33; 07 W3); SageMaker Debugger is closed to new customers and Profiler reaches end of support on 2027-06-30 (T07-34, STALE). Holistic Trace Analysis (v0.6.1) reads one PyTorch profiler trace per rank and reports, per rank, time split into compute, non-compute and idle, idle time split into host wait, kernel wait and other, the share of communication overlapped with compute, and ranks whose communication kernels start late (U07-5 closed). Start NCCL logging at `NCCL_DEBUG=WARN`; for bring-up use `INFO` with `NCCL_DEBUG_SUBSYS` and a per-rank `NCCL_DEBUG_FILE`, and profile with Nsight Systems over a capture window, one report per rank; NVIDIA publishes no overhead figure for these settings, so measure a step with and without (U07-6, accept-as-hedge). On Trainium the Neuron runtime times out a hung execution after 30 s by default (`NEURON_RT_EXEC_TIMEOUT`), exits with `NRT_TIMEOUT` or `NRT_EXEC_HW_ERR_COLLECTIVES` on a collective hang, and writes a runtime core dump per device; no Neuron counterpart to `torchfrtrace`'s cross-rank matching was located in the Neuron 2.33 runtime documentation read (U07-8). Practical value, and the ending: a per-path list of what to switch on (the HyperPod add-on's training metrics, the full DCGM counter set, Container Insights enhanced observability, a flight-recorder dump directory on shared storage) and the one thing never to switch off (the collective timeout).
