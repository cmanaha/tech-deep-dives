### Layer B. One accelerator: the step, its arithmetic, its memory

**3. What one training step computes, and what it keeps**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Name every byte and every FLOP of a step before you split any of them.

Introduces the step as three passes and one update. The forward pass costs about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token and the backward pass about 4 (a gradient for the layer input and one for the weights), so a step costs about 6N FLOPs per token (T01-14). The update then reads the gradients and the optimizer state and writes new weights. Two kinds of state follow, and the whole dive turns on the difference: model state (weights, gradients, optimizer state) persists from step to step and scales with parameters; activations live only from a micro-batch's forward pass to its backward pass and scale with tokens in flight (T01-10, T01-11). Defines micro-batch, gradient accumulation and global batch with one identity, B = b x m x d (micro-batch x accumulation steps x data-parallel size), because every later sizing decision uses it (T01-52). Mixed precision is introduced here only as "compute in 16 bits, keep an FP32 master copy for the update", with the reason: an update more than about 2,048 times smaller than its FP16 weight vanishes when added (T01-39, T01-41); section 6 develops formats. Mental model: a step is a fixed bill in FLOPs and a fixed bill in bytes, and distributed training is the art of splitting both. Diagram: one step as a timeline (forward, backward, optimizer) with the two kinds of state drawn as bars: model state constant across the step, activations rising through the forward pass and falling through the backward pass.

**4. Counting the arithmetic: 6ND, MFU and how long a run takes**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Estimate a run's accelerator-hours from three numbers, and know what the estimate leaves out.

Builds the training-time identity: time = 6ND / (accelerators x dense peak x MFU) (T01-58; Megatron-LM prints the recomputation variant 8TP/(nX), T01-23). Three corrections make it honest. Attention adds 12Lhs FLOPs per token on top of 6N, so the rule undercounts as sequences grow: for Llama 3.1 8B attention is 12.5% (causal kernel) to 22% (no causal discount) of per-token FLOPs at 8,192 tokens and passes half at about 57K (causal) or 29K tokens [derived, 01 W6] (T01-17, T01-18, C01-2). The N in 6N is the matmul parameter count, which excludes the embedding table: using the full count overstates the 8B model's FLOPs by 7% [derived, 01 W1]. Recomputation adds hardware FLOPs that MFU deliberately does not count; HFU counts them, which is why Megatron-LM's "52% of peak" and Llama 3's 38% to 43% MFU are different quantities (T01-19, T01-21, T01-22, C01-5). MFU divides by the dense peak at the training precision; NVIDIA's pages print the 2:4 sparse figure, twice the dense one, so an H100's BF16 denominator is 989.5 TFLOPS, not 1,979 (T01-27, T01-29). The check against a real run: 6ND for Llama 3 405B is 3.80e25 FLOPs, Meta's stated budget, and at the run's own measured 380 to 430 TFLOPs per GPU it accounts for 80% to 90% of the 30.84M published GPU-hours [derived, 01 W7] (T01-5, T01-6, T01-7). The section's strongest number, landed twice: a real run takes 1.1 to 1.3 times the 6ND estimate at its own throughput. Published MFU for context: PaLM 45.7% (T01-20), MegaScale 55.2% on 12,288 GPUs with model and optimizer changes contributing part of the gain (T01-24, T01-25), Llama 3 405B 38% to 43% (T01-6); DeepSeek-V3 reports GPU-hours but no MFU (T01-26). Scaling-law intuition enters only as far as it sets D: Chinchilla-optimal is about 20 tokens per parameter (T01-56), and production models are over-trained on purpose because they are served many times (Llama 3.1 8B saw about 1,870 tokens per parameter, T01-57). Practical value, and the ending: **Calculator A, run time and accelerator-hours**, with model, tokens, instance, accelerator count and an MFU slider (default 40%, inside Llama 3's measured range, labelled as an assumption), showing the 01 W8 table as presets (70B on 1T tokens: about 297,000 H100-hours, 48 days on 32 p5.48xlarge [derived]) and a cost line that reads section 21's dated rates. Diagram: per-token FLOPs as a stacked bar (dense 6N, attention) across sequence lengths 2K to 128K for 8B and 70B.

**5. The memory budget: 16 bytes per parameter, then activations**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Know whether a model fits, and which lever buys back memory at what price.

Model state first. Mixed-precision Adam holds 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for BF16 weights, 2 for gradients, and 12 for the optimizer's FP32 master weights and two moments (T01-9, T02-5). Llama 3.1 8B therefore needs 128.5 GB of model state before a single activation, 1.6 times an 80 GB H100; 70B needs 1,128.9 GB [derived, 01 W2]. The optimizer owns the memory, and alternative optimizers change the 12: DeepSeek-V3's BF16 moments with FP32 master and gradients (14 bytes total), 8-bit Adam (10), Muon (12), Adafactor without momentum (about 8); the FP32 master copy survives every row (T01-47, T01-59, T01-60, T01-61; framework layouts not read, U01-12). Activations second. Per layer they are about 34 sbh bytes for Llama's layer with a FlashAttention kernel, our derivation from Korthikanti et al.'s method (T01-31, T01-38; 01 W3, U01-11); with eager attention the 5as/h term makes them ten times larger. Llama 3.1 8B at 8,192 tokens and micro-batch 1 holds 36.5 GB of activations across 32 layers, plus 4.2 GB of FP32 logits from its 128,256-entry vocabulary, which a GPT-era rule of thumb calls negligible and which here is 11.5% of the layer total [derived, 01 W4] (T01-35, C01-1, C01-3). The levers, each with its price: sharding model state over data-parallel ranks (section 8), tensor plus sequence parallelism (section 9, divides activations by t), full recomputation (stores 2sbh per layer for about 33% more FLOPs), selective recomputation (single-digit percent), and a smaller micro-batch (T01-12, T01-32 to T01-34, T01-36, T01-37). Pipeline parallelism is the lever that does not cut peak activations: the first stage holds L layers' worth whatever p is (T01-62, T02-19). Beyond the formula sit temporary buffers and fragmentation (T01-11, T01-63): TorchTitan's measured 70B peak was 67.6 to 71.9 GiB where the formula gives about 39 GB (02 section 3.2, T02-50, U02-8), so formula totals are lower bounds. Practical value, and the ending: **Calculator B, the memory budget**, with model, optimizer row, sharding stage and degree, TP with or without SP, recomputation mode, micro-batch and sequence length, returning model state, activations, logits and the headroom left on the chosen accelerator, with every value above 60 GB of 80 flagged "confirm with a measured step" [our reading; no source states a headroom figure]. Diagram: a stacked bar per configuration (01 W5's rows) against an 80 GB and a 141 GB line.

**6. Precision for training: BF16, FP8 and the microscaled formats**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Pick a training precision your accelerator runs in its matrix units, and know what each step down costs.

Opens with the mental model: lower precision is a story about where the scale factor lives. FP16 needs one loss scale for the whole run (T01-40); BF16 has FP32's range and needs none (T01-41); FP8 needs a scale per tensor, E4M3 for weights and activations and E5M2 for gradients in the original proposal (T01-42, T01-44); DeepSeek-V3 used E4M3 everywhere with a scale per 1x128 tile of activations and per 128x128 block of weights (T01-46, C01-7); MXFP8 shares a power-of-two scale across 32 values (T01-48); NVFP4 uses an FP8 scale per 16 values plus one per tensor (T01-49). Each step down moves the scale closer to the data. What it costs, from first-party runs: FP8 matched BF16 within run-to-run noise up to GPT 175B in its proposers' runs (T01-43); DeepSeek-V3 reports a relative loss error under 0.25% against BF16 on validation runs of about 1T tokens on two smaller models (T01-47); NVIDIA's NVFP4 run on a 12B hybrid model tracked FP8 within 1% relative loss, widening past 1.5% during learning-rate decay, with 16% of linear layers kept in BF16 (T01-50). What each accelerator runs: Transformer Engine needs compute capability 8.9 for FP8, 9.0 for FP8 block scaling, and Blackwell (10.0) for MXFP8 and NVFP4, and picks MXFP8 by default on Blackwell (T01-45); FP8 on H100 therefore runs through Transformer Engine in Megatron Core, Accelerate or NeMo AutoModel, since TorchTitan removed its Float8 path in v0.4.0 and now offers MXFP8 and NVFP4 on Blackwell only (T03-13, T03-19, T03-41, STALE); Trainium2's Tensor Engine takes cFP8, BF16, FP16, TF32 and FP32 with hardware stochastic rounding (T01-51), yet the PyTorch-native Neuron stack trains Trn2 in BF16 only, with FP8 and MXFP8 gated on Trainium3 (T03-36, T03-53, C03-10). The FP32 master copy survives every format (T01-39, T01-47, T01-48), which is why precision changes the compute bill far more than the memory bill. Practical value, and the ending: a row per reference accelerator with the formats its matrix units run for training and the recipe name to set, linking the silicon dive's precision section for inference formats. Diagram: the scale granularity ladder (run, tensor, tile, 32-value block, 16-value block) as nested rectangles over one weight matrix.

### Layer C. Many accelerators: splitting the step

**7. Collectives: five operations and what each one costs**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Price any parallelism in bytes per link before you run it.

Introduces the vocabulary every later section uses. All-reduce, reduce-scatter, all-gather, all-to-all and point-to-point send and receive, each defined by what every rank holds before and after. The identity that explains half of the dive: an all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather, so a bandwidth-optimal all-reduce moves 2(n-1)/n of the data per rank, and reduce-scatter, all-gather and all-to-all each move (n-1)/n (T02-4, T02-60, T02-61). Algorithm bandwidth against bus bandwidth, as nccl-tests reports them, so the reader can read a benchmark (T02-60). Ring against tree: the same bandwidth, latency linear against logarithmic in rank count (T02-62). The bandwidth hierarchy on p5.48xlarge, per GPU per direction: 450 GB/s on NVLink against 50 GB/s on EFA, a factor of 9 [derived, 02 section 3.4] (T02-68, T02-70); DeepSeek's H800 cluster had a ratio of 3.2 (T02-39). The time estimate the procedure in section 14 uses: bytes over per-GPU link bandwidth, compared against compute time; achieved bus bandwidth sits below these link peaks (U02-9). Configuration of NCCL on EFA belongs to the EFA dive's NCCL over EFA section, which this section links. Mental model: every parallelism trades a replicated or full-size object for a collective, and the collective's bytes times its frequency decide which link it can live on. Diagram: the five collectives as before-and-after rank grids, with the reduce-scatter plus all-gather decomposition of all-reduce drawn explicitly.

**8. Data parallelism and sharding: DDP, ZeRO, FSDP and HSDP**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Shard model state across data-parallel ranks, and keep the sharding on the fast link.

Data parallelism first: every rank holds the full model and averages gradients with one all-reduce per step, which PyTorch DDP overlaps with the backward pass by launching it bucket by bucket (T02-1 to T02-3; DDP's current default bucket size unchecked, U02-1). Its bytes per step are 2 x Psi per rank, independent of how many ranks there are (T02-4). ZeRO then removes the redundancy in three stages: optimizer state (4 + 12/d bytes per parameter per rank), plus gradients (2 + 14/d), plus parameters (16/d) (T02-6, 01 W2). Stages 1 and 2 cost no extra bytes, because an all-reduce already was a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather; stage 3 adds one all-gather, 3 x Psi per step, 1.5 times DP (T02-7). FSDP generalises stage 3 with a sharding factor F between 1 (DDP) and the world size, and hybrid sharding (HSDP) maps each sharding group to one instance, so all-gathers and reduce-scatters stay on NVLink and only an all-reduce of each GPU's shard crosses EFA (T02-8 to T02-10; the paper's own F notation is inconsistent, C02-1, U02-13). ZeRO++ attacks the same cross-node bytes with quantised gathers and a secondary in-node copy (T02-11). The worked example carries the section: Llama 3.1 8B on four p5.48xlarge, flat FSDP over 32 GPUs can move up to about 373 GB per GPU per step across EFA if parameters are re-gathered on every accumulation micro-step, while HSDP with one instance per sharding group moves about 3 GB, a factor of about 120 [derived, 02 section 3.3]. The high end is the default: FSDP2 reduce-scatters and re-gathers on every accumulation micro-batch unless the trainer turns sync off, TorchTitan keeps that default, and from PyTorch 2.15 an unset `reduce_dtype` reduces FP32 gradients in FP32, which raises the same step to about 498 GB; deferring the reduction to the last micro-batch brings it to about 156 GB at the price of a full unsharded gradient per rank (T03-2 to T03-4, C03-9) [derived, 03 W1]. Data parallelism has a ceiling that is not a link: past the critical batch size more replicas waste compute, and at a fixed global batch more DP ranks shrink each rank's batch, which Llama 3 names as the reason its MFU fell from 43% to 41% going from 8,192 to 16,384 GPUs (T01-53 to T01-55). Mental model: cross the slow link once, fan out on the fast one. Practical value, and the ending: shard optimizer state first, keep the sharding group inside the instance, and treat the global batch as the cap on DP. Diagram: DDP, ZeRO-1, ZeRO-3 and HSDP drawn on two instances of eight GPUs, with each collective coloured by the link it rides.

**9. Tensor and sequence parallelism: splitting every layer**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Split each layer across the GPUs of one instance, and see why that is where tensor parallelism stops.

Megatron's column-then-row split lets the MLP's nonlinearity run locally and needs one all-reduce per block in the forward pass and one in the backward; attention splits by heads (T02-12). Its traffic scales with activations, not parameters: 8bsh(t-1)/t elements per layer per micro-batch (T02-13). For Llama 3.1 70B at TP 8 and 8,192 tokens that is 0.94 GB per layer per micro-batch, 2.1 ms on NVLink against 13.3 ms of layer compute, about 16% if none of it overlaps [derived, 02 section 3.2]. The frequency is the reason for Megatron's first takeaway, TP up to the GPUs of one server and no further (T02-14). The divisibility rules a framework enforces: heads divide by t, and KV heads (query groups under grouped-query attention) are a multiple or divisor of t (T02-15). Sequence parallelism splits the layer norms and dropout that TP leaves replicated along the sequence and swaps each all-reduce for a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather: the same bytes, so it costs nothing in volume, though the paper measures the pair as slower than one all-reduce; its value is the activation memory it divides by t (T02-16 to T02-18, C02-8). Mental model: frequency matters as much as size; TP fires inside every layer of every micro-batch, which pins it to the fastest link. Practical value, and the ending: TP no larger than the GPUs in one instance (8 on p5), always paired with SP, and checked against the head counts. Diagram: one transformer layer split across four GPUs, the f and g operators marked, and the per-layer bytes beside the NVLink and EFA bandwidth bars.

**10. Pipeline parallelism and the bubble**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Size the bubble before you add stages, and know which memory pipelining does not save.

Stages hold consecutive layers; micro-batches flow through them; the idle time at the start and end of each step is the bubble. GPipe's bubble over total time is (p-1)/(m+p-1) (T02-27); over ideal time it is (p-1)/m (T02-28), and the two denominators make quoted percentages incomparable unless stated (C02-9). 1F1B keeps the same bubble and caps in-flight micro-batches at p (T02-29); interleaving v chunks per device divides the bubble by v at v times the pipeline traffic and needs m to be a multiple of p (T02-30); Zero Bubble splits the backward into input and weight gradients to fill the gaps (T02-33); DeepSeek's DualPipe runs micro-batches from both ends and overlaps compute with all-to-all (T02-34); Llama 3 decoupled micro-batch count from stage count and moved a layer off the first and last stages to balance embedding and loss work (T02-35). Pipeline traffic is small: bsh/t per micro-batch per boundary, 16.8 MB for the 70B example, 0.34 ms on EFA against 266 ms of stage compute, which is why PP is the dimension that crosses instances (T02-31, 02 section 3.2). The memory fact a newcomer gets wrong: under 1F1B the first stage holds L layers' worth of activations whatever p is, so PP divides weights and optimizer state, not peak activations (T02-19, T01-62). Megatron called activation recomputation "required" with pipelines in 2021; Llama 3 trained 405B with PP 16 at 8K without it (T02-32, STALE, C02-11). Worked bubble: 70B on 256 H100 at TP 8 x PP 4 x DP 8, micro-batch 1, gives m = 64 and a 1F1B bubble of 4.7%, and interleaving with v = 5 brings it to 0.94% [derived, 02 section 3.2]. Mental model: the bubble is (p-1)/(v x m), and the global batch caps m. Practical value, and the ending: the smallest p that fits, then raise m or v until the bubble is near one percent. Diagram: an interactive schedule chart (GPipe, 1F1B, interleaved) for p = 4 and a slider for m, with the bubble shaded.

**11. Long sequences: context parallelism**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Split one long sequence across GPUs, and pick ring, all-gather or all-to-all by your attention layout.

Context parallelism splits every activation along the sequence, unlike sequence parallelism, which splits only the norms and dropout (T02-25). Attention needs every token's keys and values, so CP pays for its split with communication of K and V. Three published designs: Ring Attention passes K and V blocks around a ring and hides the transfer behind block compute when the block is long enough, c >= F/B (T02-20, T02-21); DeepSpeed-Ulysses uses two all-to-alls so each GPU holds the full sequence for a subset of heads, which bounds its degree by the head count (T02-22, T02-23, U02-2); Llama 3 all-gathers K and V and accepts the exposed transfer because grouped-query attention makes K and V small, with a two-chunks-per-rank layout that balances the causal mask (T02-24). On an H100, the ring's overlap condition gives about 2.2K tokens per block over NVLink and about 19.8K over EFA before GQA's 8x reduction [derived, 02 section 3.4]. CP replicates weights, so its ranks fold into the data-parallel gradient reduction and it saves no parameter memory (T02-26). Llama 3's 128K stage used CP 16 at 38% MFU (T02-47). Mental model: long sequences hide their own communication, because attention compute grows with s squared while CP traffic grows with s. Practical value, and the ending: add CP at 8K tokens and above (Megatron-Core's guidance, T02-25, T02-42), and choose the variant by head layout and mask. Diagram: the three variants side by side on four GPUs, with the bytes each moves per attention layer.

**12. Mixture of experts: expert parallelism**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Place experts so the all-to-all stays inside the fast domain, and size the expert grid separately from the dense one.

Introduces the mixture-of-experts layer in two sentences (a router sends each token to k of E experts) and links the silicon dive's MoE section for why sparsity exists. Expert parallelism places experts on different GPUs and moves tokens to them with an all-to-all dispatch and an all-to-all combine per MoE layer, forward and backward; the bytes are k copies of each token's hidden state each way (T02-37, T02-38). Capacity factor and dropped tokens (T02-36). DeepSeek-V3's answer to a slow cross-node link: route each token to at most 4 nodes, cross InfiniBand once per node, fan out on NVLink, and run 64-way EP across 8 nodes with PP 16 and no TP on 2,048 H800s (T02-39, T02-40); per token per MoE layer that is about 172 KB of forward dispatch-plus-combine before de-duplication [derived, 02 section 3.4]. Megatron-Core's MoE parallel folding gives attention and expert layers separate grids that share only PP, and its own guide's "Total GPUs = TP x PP x CP x EP x DP" disagrees with the code, which factorises the two grids separately (T02-41, T02-72 WRONG, C02-2). Mental model: an MoE model runs two parallel layouts in one model. Practical value, and the ending: keep EP x expert-TP inside the NVLink domain, prefer EP over TP for fine-grained experts, and check the world size against both grids (T02-42). Diagram: dispatch and combine for one token across two instances, with node-limited routing drawn as one InfiniBand or EFA hop and an NVLink fan-out.

**13. Composing the dimensions: how frontier runs are laid out**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Read any published training layout as a nesting order and a reason for each degree.

The published layouts, read with the previous six sections. Llama 3 405B: TP 8 x CP 1 x PP 16 x DP 64 on 8,192 GPUs at 43% MFU, DP 128 on 16,384 at 41%, CP 16 at 131K tokens at 38%, nested [TP, CP, PP, DP] from innermost to outermost because the innermost needs the most bandwidth and least latency (T02-46, T02-47). DeepSeek-V3: no TP, PP 16, EP 64, ZeRO-1 DP (T02-40). TorchTitan: FSDP2, TP, PP and CP composed on one device mesh, with FSDP alone recommended up to about 512 GPUs (T02-49, T02-50; its paper and its repository benchmarks disagree on numbers, C02-3). Megatron-Core: default rank order "tp-cp-ep-dp-pp", with adjacent ranks expected to share a node (T02-51, C02-4). Megatron's PTD-P takeaways: model-parallel size just large enough to fit, then scale with DP; its layout beat ZeRO-3 without model parallelism by 70% at 175B and 530B (T02-43 to T02-45). Llama 3's FP32 gradient accumulation and reduce-scatter, and its internal memory and performance projection tools, show what a frontier team builds around the layout (T02-48). Mental model: nest innermost-fastest, TP then CP then PP then DP, with the innermost inside one instance; Llama 3, TorchTitan and Megatron-Core agree. Practical value, and the ending: given any layout, the reader can say which link each dimension rides and why its degree was chosen, which section 14 turns into a procedure. Diagram: the 4D mesh as nested boxes, Llama 3's degrees filled in, each level labelled with its collective and link.

**14. Set my ranks: the sizing procedure and calculator**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Turn a model, a cluster and a global batch into two or three layouts worth measuring.

The dive's operational centre. A 12-step procedure assembled from the published ones (02 section 3.5; [our reading] where no single source states a step): gather inputs; check 16 x Psi against HBM; shard model state over DP before adding model parallelism (ZeRO-1 or the distributed optimizer first, then ZeRO-2, then FSDP; HSDP when cross-instance bytes dominate); add TP up to the instance size with SP; add the smallest PP that fits; add CP for long sequences; set the expert grid separately for MoE; satisfy the divisibility constraints of both grids; check the bubble; check bytes per link against compute; nest innermost-fastest; measure the survivors for a few hundred steps (T02-5, T02-6, T02-9, T02-14, T02-15, T02-19, T02-25, T02-30, T02-41, T02-46, T02-52, T02-54, T02-57, T02-58). What the published tools do and where they disagree: NeMo 2.x's auto-configurator searched TP, PP, CP, EP and micro-batch and measured (T02-54, C02-6), and was not located in any current NeMo-family repository, the NVIDIA/NeMo repository having become speech-only at v3.0.0 (T03-25, T03-27, U03-2); DeepSpeed's autotuner walks ZeRO stages and micro-batch only (T02-56); the Ultra-Scale Playbook's fit-then-batch-then-optimise steps (T02-57, Tier 3); and for Llama 3.1 8B on one node NeMo starts at TP 2 or 4, TorchTitan at FSDP 8 and NVIDIA's guide at CP 2 (T02-55). Two worked examples carry the section. Llama 3.1 70B on 32 p5.48xlarge at 8,192 tokens and a 4.2M-token batch: TP 8 x PP 4 x DP 8 with ZeRO-1 and interleaving (about 38 GB per GPU on paper, bubble about 1%, DP traffic about 1% of a 17.5 s step) and TP 8 x FSDP 32 (TorchTitan's published layout, FSDP traffic about 6%, closest to the 80 GB limit in measurement) both survive [derived, 02 section 3.2] (T02-50). A third published layout for the same model on 32 H100, Megatron Bridge's recipe at TP 4 x PP 4 x VP 5 x CP 2, holds 52.9 GB of model state per GPU with the distributed optimizer and 79.4 GB without it, which is why the recipe turns it on (T03-24, T03-17) [derived, 03 W2]. Llama 3.1 8B: FSDP 8 on one instance; HSDP with one instance per sharding group on four [derived, 02 section 3.3]. Practical value, and the ending: **Calculator C, parallelism sizing**, which takes a model (presets for Llama 3.1 8B, 70B, 405B and DeepSeek-V3, or custom shapes), an instance type and count, a sequence length and global batch, enumerates layouts that pass divisibility and Calculator B's memory check, and ranks them by bubble and by bytes per link as a share of compute, ending on "measure these two or three". An agent can call the same procedure. Diagram: the procedure as a decision flow, each step labelled with its check and its source.

### Layer F (draft part). Running it on AWS: who owns the cluster around the step

**23. Three shapes of a training cluster on AWS, and who owns what**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Know which parts of a cluster AWS runs on each path, and which parts stay yours on every path.

Introduces the AWS half of the dive with one map before any service detail. Three shapes: a job-scoped cluster that exists for one job and bills for its duration (SageMaker training jobs, T06-23); a long-lived Slurm cluster (self-managed on EC2, AWS PCS, HyperPod Slurm; T06-3, T06-31, T06-38); and a long-lived Kubernetes cluster (self-managed EKS, AWS Batch on EKS, HyperPod EKS; T06-2, T06-41, T06-49). The ownership matrix from register 06 section 3.1 is the section's diagram: scheduler and gang admission, node health checks, node replacement, job restart, capacity, node image, fabric setup, Trainium, cost beyond the instance, status markers, each cell cited to its entry. Three mental models carry it: node recovery is not job recovery, because health agents replace a bad node but only a job-level mechanism plus a checkpoint the job wrote itself gets training running again (T06-4, T06-6, T06-7, T06-43); synchronous training needs all-or-nothing admission, which Slurm has built in and Kubernetes adds through a gang scheduler (T06-44, T06-47, T06-50); and a cluster per job bills per job and pays a provisioning wait each time, while a cluster per team bills for its whole life, idle included (T06-18, T06-23, T06-26, T06-36). Practical value, and the ending: whatever the path, the training script owns three things, writing and reloading checkpoints, saving before a Capacity Block or training plan window closes (EC2 begins terminating instances 30 minutes before the end), and logging progress every step so a watchdog can see a hang (T06-7, T06-12, T06-27, T06-30, T06-34, T06-42). Diagram: the ownership matrix as a grid coloured AWS-managed, customer, or not read, with the three shapes as column groups.

**24. The Slurm family: your own Slurm, AWS PCS and HyperPod Slurm**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Pick the Slurm path by who runs the controller and who checks the GPUs.

Slurm is the scheduler most large training teams already know, and AWS offers it three ways. Self-managed on EC2, with AWS ParallelCluster as the deployment tool: still maintained at v3.16.2 and supported to 2028-02-20, while every page of its user guide now opens with a banner recommending PCS (T06-38). AWS PCS: AWS runs and patches the Slurm controller, the customer brings an AMI carrying the PCS agent (a PCS-ready DLAMI exists for GPU), cluster size is fixed at creation (Small up to 32 instances, Medium 512, Large 2,048), Slurm majors move yearly (25.05 reaches PCS end of life on 2026-11-30), and GPU health checks and requeue remain the customer's (T06-31 to T06-35, T06-37). Practical value, and the ending: a Slurm team that wants AWS to own node health picks HyperPod Slurm; one that wants its own images and only a managed controller picks PCS and budgets the fee and the yearly upgrade; ParallelCluster stays for clusters already running. ### Layer E. Keeping a run alive

**17. Failure is the steady state: interruption rates and goodput**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Predict how often your job will stop from its node count, before you design anything to survive it.

Opens with the mental model: a synchronous job is a series system. It stops when any node stops, so its mean time to failure is the node's divided by the node count, MTTF = 1 / (N_nodes x r_f) (T04-7). Meta measured r_f at 2.34 and 6.50 failures per thousand node-days on its two research clusters, with a bad month near 17.5, and job MTTF falling from 47.7 days for 8-GPU jobs to 7.9 hours for 1,024-GPU jobs (T04-7, T04-8). The same rate gives the reference 256-GPU job a hardware interruption every 2 to 13 days and a 16,384-GPU job one every 1 to 5 hours [derived, 04 W1]; an order-of-magnitude planning aid, because the paper's counting rules differ between its two measures. The first-party reports agree on the shape. Llama 3 405B saw 466 interruptions in 54 days, 419 unexpected, one every 3.1 hours, about 78% confirmed or suspected hardware, and still kept more than 90% effective training time (T04-1, T04-2, T04-4; Table 5's first row is internally inconsistent, so the page quotes counts, C04-1, U04-1). OPT-175B logged about 90 restarts over its final lineage, mostly hardware, and stalled on checkpoint downloads that hung (T04-17, T04-18). MegaScale restarted over 100 times in several weeks on more than 10,000 GPUs and recovered over 90% of faults automatically (T04-13). Meta's research clusters: removing 40 "lemon" nodes, 1.2% of the fleet, cut the failure rate of 512-GPU-plus jobs from 14% to 4% (T04-12), and hardware failures touched under 1% of jobs but 19% of GPU runtime (T04-11). Effective training time ratio (ETTR, productive time over wallclock) is the goodput measure the dive uses, and about 90% is what the best operators reach at 10K GPUs with heavy automation (T04-1, T04-9, T04-11, T04-13). AWS's HyperPod pages state "60-80% typical goodput on self-managed clusters" and over 95% with checkpointless training; these are vendor claims without stated conditions and are shown labelled, apart from the operator reports (T04-52, C04-5). The section's strongest number, landed twice: one failure every 3.1 hours on Llama 3's 16K GPUs. Practical value, and the ending: read your node count across the W1 table to get the interruption interval your checkpoint and restart design must beat, and screen nodes before they join (section 24's deep health checks; Meta's lemon-node lesson). Diagram: MTTF against node count on log axes, one line per measured r_f, with the 256-GPU and 16K-GPU points marked.

**18. Checkpointing: what to save, how often, how fast, and where**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Pick a checkpoint interval and a storage size from your node count and your save time.

What a checkpoint holds: weights and optimizer state (most of the bytes), plus the data-loader position, RNG states, scheduler and step counter, which are kilobytes to megabytes and decide whether a resumed run is the same run (T04-23, T04-35). Gradients are not saved. That makes 12 to 14 bytes per parameter: about 988 GB for Llama 3.1 70B at 14, 3.86 GB per GPU on 256 GPUs [derived, 04 W2]; cross-checks from real runs: OPT-175B wrote 1.6 TB in 992 files, Llama 3 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU (T04-5, T04-18). How often: the Young and Daly interval, dt* = sqrt(2 w_cp / (N_nodes r_f)), balances the time a save blocks against the work lost per failure (T04-9; the primary papers sit behind publisher access and the formula is cited through Meta's study, T04-10, U04-2). For the 70B job on 256 GPUs: 2.0 hours for a 60 s blocking save, 21.5 minutes for an asynchronous save that blocks about 2 s, with expected ETTR 0.982 against 0.996; at 16K GPUs the same arithmetic gives 0.79 against 0.93, and restart time dominates [derived, 04 W3]. How fast: aggregate write bandwidth is bytes over blocking time, 16.5 GB/s for a 60 s save of the 70B checkpoint, which on FSx for Lustre is bought with capacity, about 16.5 TiB at PERSISTENT-1000, because per-client ceilings (100 Gbps without EFA, 700 with, 1,200 with GPUDirect Storage) are far above the per-node need (T04-29, T04-30, T05-35, T05-36) [derived, 04 W2, 05 section 3.5]. On S3, the request rate per prefix sets the floor and prefix strategies exist for it (T04-26, T04-27, U04-8); S3 Express One Zone's per-bucket rates change the picture (T04-28). Asynchronous and tiered designs: PyTorch DCP saves in parallel from every rank and reshards at load (T04-19); `async_save` called bare stages synchronously into pageable memory, so the fast path needs an explicit pinned-memory stager, which TorchTitan's `async_with_pinned_mem` mode sets (T04-20 to T04-22, C04-2); Megatron's `--async-save` is silently turned off unless `--use-persistent-ckpt-worker` is also set (T04-38, C04-4); MegaScale and Gemini copy to host memory and to a peer's memory first, with storage as the backstop (T04-14, T04-33, T04-34); HyperPod managed tiered checkpointing (EKS, GA 2025-09-08) does the same as a managed feature, and its published example initialises an NCCL-only process group that PyTorch's `async_save` rejects, a code reading not yet tested (T04-31, T04-32, C04-3, U04-4). Mental model: save is asynchronous, load is not. Practical value, and the ending: **Calculator D, checkpoint interval and storage**, which takes model size, node count, a failure rate (presets 2.34, 6.50, 17.5), save mode and restart time, and returns the interval in steps, expected ETTR, and the FSx capacity or S3 layout the save time needs. Diagram: a step timeline with synchronous and asynchronous saves drawn against a failure, showing lost work and blocked time.

**19. Resuming correctly: restart, elasticity, stragglers and determinism**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Make a restart resume the same run, and find the slow rank before it costs you a tenth of the cluster.

Restart mechanics, from the launcher up: `torchrun` restarts every worker when any one fails, up to `--max-restarts`, which defaults to 0, and ranks are not stable across restarts (T04-40); Slurm's `--requeue` reruns the batch script under the same job ID and `--signal` warns a job before its end (T04-41); HyperPod adds node replacement and auto-resume around both (section 24 and 25, T06-6, T06-7, T06-9). Elastic training changes only the data-parallel dimension, because tensor and pipeline layouts are baked into each rank's shard: HyperPod elastic training and TorchFT add or drop whole replicas with the global batch held, and TorchFT recovers a replica from its healthy peers per step instead of from storage (T04-42, T06-13, T06-14). Determinism: data order should be a function, not a history; Megatron derives document, sample and shuffle indices from a seed and checkpoints only a counter, and `torchdata`'s `StatefulDataLoader` checkpoints batches yielded (T04-44, T04-45; MosaicML Streaming's elastic determinism is a vendor claim, T04-46). Bitwise reproducibility is not guaranteed across releases or platforms and costs speed; TorchTitan's determinism switch sets the knobs and logs the slowdown (T04-24, T04-43; NCCL's run-to-run determinism open, U04-5). Stragglers cost as much as failures and hide better: a ByteDance trace of 3,079 jobs found 42.5% straggling, 10.4% of GPU-hours lost, faulty machines explaining most of the slowdown in only 1.7% of straggling jobs, the rest software and workload imbalance such as unbalanced pipeline stages and sequence lengths (T04-47, T04-48); MegaScale gained 0.7% MFU by removing hosts about 10% slower (T04-15); Llama 3 found stragglers by ranking suspect collectives (T04-6); Alibaba's fail-slow episodes ran from seconds to ten hours (T04-49). Mental model: the waiting shows up in collectives, so a straggler is found by per-rank timing of compute, not by the rank that is waiting. Practical value, and the ending: set `--max-restarts` above 0 or use the orchestrator's resume, checkpoint the data-loader state, derive data order from a seed, and log per-rank step time so section 29's straggler check has its input. Diagram: one step across eight ranks with one slow rank, showing the other seven waiting inside the all-reduce.

**22. Storage for training on AWS: data in, checkpoints out**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Size training storage for the checkpoint burst and the restart read, because the tokens barely register.

The arithmetic that sets the design first: the 70B job on 256 H100 reads about 1 MB/s of tokens in total; Meta's 405B run read about 11 MB/s from a storage fabric that sustained 2 TB/s [derived, 04 W5] (T04-5, T04-50). Storage is the bottleneck at four moments instead: the checkpoint save burst, the restart read, start-up metadata storms when thousands of ranks list or open objects, and non-text data (T04-27, T04-51; [our reading], 04 W5). Then the services, each with its documented limits. FSx for Lustre: Persistent 2 is the only deployment type with EFA; throughput is bought per TiB (125 to 1,000 MBps per TiB on SSD); per-client ceilings of 100 Gbps without EFA, 700 with EFA, 1,200 with GPUDirect Storage; EFA chosen at creation, clients in the same Availability Zone, and trn2 excluded from the EFA path; a data repository association lazy-loads from S3 on first access (T05-35 to T05-38, T04-29, T04-30; the FSx-on-EFA mechanism is the EFA dive's StorageDataPaths section). Amazon S3: 3,500 writes and 5,500 reads per second per partitioned prefix, scaling gradually with 503 Slow Down while it scales (T05-39, T04-27); S3 Express One Zone directory buckets at up to 200,000 reads and 100,000 writes per second (T05-40). The Amazon S3 Connector for PyTorch v1.5.0 implements DCP's writer and reader for checkpoints and iterable and map datasets for data, with one I/O thread per rank by default (T04-25, T04-26, T04-51). Mountpoint for Amazon S3 reads large objects at high throughput and writes sequentially by one writer, which suits data but limits checkpoints (T05-41). Instance store: 8 x 3,800 GB NVMe on P5 and P6, ephemeral (T05-42). Host memory as a tier: one 70B checkpoint shard is 30.9 GB per p5.48xlarge against 2,048 GiB of host memory (04 W2, T05-2). Mental model: text tokens are cheap; checkpoints are not. Practical value, and the ending: a pre-tokenised, memory-mapped corpus on FSx (or streamed from S3) plus a checkpoint path sized by Calculator D, with S3 as the durable copy. ### Layer D. The software that implements it

**15. The framework map: engines, front ends and maturity labels**
Depth: survey
Payoff: Choose a training framework by the engine underneath it, at a pinned version, after reading its maturity label.

Opens with the mental model that organises the field: a handful of engines implement parallelism (PyTorch's FSDP2 and DTensor, Megatron Core, DeepSpeed), and everything else is a front end that picks a configuration and a loop (TorchTitan, Megatron Bridge, NeMo AutoModel, Accelerate, the transformers Trainer, and every post-training framework), so choosing a framework is mostly choosing an engine (T03-16, T03-23, T03-26, T03-42, T03-50). Nanotron enters only as the code behind the Ultra-Scale Playbook (U03-9). Practical value, and the ending: a three-question chooser (which accelerator and precision, which model source, how much of the stack you want to read) that points at an engine and a front end, with the version to pin. **16. Fine-tuning and post-training runs as systems**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Size a fine-tuning or RL run's memory and pick the engine it should reuse.

Fine-tuning reuses everything above with one change to the memory budget: freezing the base weights removes their gradients and optimizer state. LoRA stores optimizer state only for small adapters, up to two thirds less training memory for a large model in its authors' words; QLoRA also quantises the frozen base to 4-bit NormalFloat and fine-tuned a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU (T03-43, T03-44). For Llama 3.1 70B before activations: full fine-tuning about 1,270 GB of model state, at least 16 H100; LoRA about 141 GB, two H100; 4-bit QLoRA about 35 GB, one [derived, 03 W4]; LLaMA-Factory's own estimated table prints 1,200, 160 and 48 GB (T03-45). The configuration-file frameworks (Axolotl v0.21.1, LLaMA-Factory v0.9.5) run these on FSDP2, DeepSpeed or Megatron Core (T03-46, T03-47). RL and preference training are inference jobs inside training jobs; the RL dive owns the algorithms and the step-as-a-system view (its section 19), and this section adds only what a systems reader needs: every post-training framework reuses a training engine (verl on FSDP, Megatron or TorchTitan; OpenRLHF on DeepSpeed; TRL on Accelerate; slime on Megatron; NeMo RL on DTensor or Megatron Bridge), so its parallelism, checkpoint and precision behaviour is the engine's (T03-49, T03-50; RL dive register 05). TorchTitan's RL stack moved out of its experimental folder in v0.4.0 (T03-15). On AWS, the HyperPod recipes at 2.0.11 are mostly fine-tuning and post-training recipes (T06-20). Practical value, and the ending: pick LoRA, QLoRA or full fine-tuning from Calculator B with the frozen-base row selected, then pick the framework by the engine you already run. **27. Trainium: the Neuron path for training**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Start a Trainium training run on the stack AWS supports today, with ranks counted the Neuron way.

Trainium enters as an accelerator family with its own software stack, introduced in the silicon dive (AwsCustomSilicon, AwsCompilersAndTooling, Neuron 2.33), which this section links for chip architecture and the compiler. What changes for training. The software moved: NxD Training reached end-of-support as of Neuron 2.32, after four different announced end dates, and AWS recommends native PyTorch with TorchTitan (T03-34, T03-35, T02-65, C03-4). TorchNeuron, the native PyTorch backend, is a public beta in Neuron 2.33.0 on Trn2 and Trn3, and TorchTitan Neuron 0.2.2+neuron.1 (Beta) runs FSDP2, TP, CP, EP and PP on them (T03-36, T03-37). A rank is a logical NeuronCore: with the default LNC=2 a trn2.48xlarge is 64 ranks, so TP 4 spans one chip and section 14's arithmetic carries over with that substitution; the validated configurations are Llama3-8B and Qwen3-32B at TP 4 x FSDP 16 on one trn2.48xlarge (T03-38) [derived: 24 GiB of HBM per logical core, 03 W3]. **28. Choosing your path**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Answer four questions and land on a path, with its fee and the work it leaves you.

The decision, assembled from the sections before it [our reading throughout; each branch cites what it rests on]. Four questions in order. (1) How long does the cluster need to exist? Many short or medium jobs and no standing cluster point to SageMaker training jobs, billed per job at 1.15 times EC2 (T06-23, T05-23); long pre-training or a shared team pool points to a long-lived cluster, billed for its whole life (T06-18, T06-36).
