## 2. Claim register

### A. How often large jobs fail, and why

**T04-1. Llama 3 405B pre-training saw 466 job interruptions in a 54-day snapshot: 47 planned (automated maintenance such as firmware upgrades, or operator actions such as configuration or dataset updates) and 419 unexpected. Meta still reports higher than 90% effective training time, with significant manual intervention needed only three times; automated maintenance alone caused at least one interruption a day.**
- Location: 2407.21783v3, Sec 3.3.4 "Reliability and Operational Challenges", first two paragraphs.
- Verdict: NEW
- Evidence: https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3 | Tier 3, preprint, first-party lab | production report | "Despite these challenges, for Llama 3, we achieved higher than 90% effective training time while supporting automated cluster maintenance, such as firmware and Linux kernel upgrades (Vigraham and Leonhardi, 2024), which resulted in at least one training interruption daily. The effective training time measures the time spent on useful training over the elapsed time. During a 54-day snapshot period of pre-training, we experienced a total of 466 job interruptions. Of these, 47 were planned interruptions due to automated maintenance operations such as firmware upgrades or operator-initiated operations like configuration or dataset updates. The remaining 419 were unexpected interruptions". "Despite the large number of failures, significant manual intervention was required only three times during this period, with the rest of issues handled by automation." (accessed 2026-10-30)
- Use in dive: the anchor number for the reliability section (mental model). At 16K-GPU scale a job is interrupted several times a day and still makes more than 90% useful progress, because restart is automated and cheap; the lesson is that failure is a rate to be engineered around, not an exception.

**T04-4. Llama 3's 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days are one every 3.1 hours, 7.8 a day [derived]. If the job ran on all 16,384 GPUs (2,048 eight-GPU servers) for the whole window, that is about 3.8 failures per thousand node-days, inside the 2.34 to 6.50 range Meta measured on its research clusters (T04-8) [derived].**
- Location: inputs from 2407.21783v3 Sec 3.3.1 ("up to 16K H100 GPUs", eight GPUs per server) and Sec 3.3.4 (419 in 54 days).
- Verdict: NEW
- Evidence: https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3 | Tier 3, preprint, first-party lab | production report (inputs) | "Llama 3 405B is trained on up to 16K H100 GPUs ... Each server is equipped with eight GPUs and two CPUs." Arithmetic [derived]: 54 days x 24 h = 1,296 h; 1,296 / 419 = 3.09 h between unexpected interruptions; 419 / 54 = 7.76 per day. Node rate: 419 / (2,048 nodes x 54 days) = 419 / 110,592 node-days = 3.79 per 1,000 node-days. Per GPU: 16,384 x 3.09 h = 50,700 GPU-hours, about 5.8 GPU-years between unexpected interruptions per GPU. The paper does not state the GPU count during the snapshot ("up to 16K"); at 8,192 GPUs the node rate doubles to 7.6 per 1,000 node-days. (accessed 2026-10-30)
- Use in dive: mental model for scaling. A per-node failure rate that looks negligible (one per 264 node-days) becomes several interruptions a day once multiplied by 2,048 nodes, because a synchronous job fails when any node fails (T04-7).

**T04-5. Llama 3's checkpoint was 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU, written to a Tectonic storage fabric of 240 PB on 7,500 SSD servers with 2 TB/s sustained and 7 TB/s peak throughput. Meta names bursty checkpoint writes that saturate the fabric as a major challenge, and set two goals: minimise GPU pause time during checkpointing and checkpoint more often to lose less work.**
- Location: 2407.21783v3, Sec 3.3.1 "Training Infrastructure", paragraph "Storage".
- Verdict: NEW
- Evidence: https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3 | Tier 3, preprint, first-party lab | production report | "It offers 240 PB of storage out of 7,500 servers equipped with SSDs, and supports a sustainable throughput of 2 TB/s and a peak throughput of 7 TB/s. A major challenge is supporting the highly bursty checkpoint writes that saturate the storage fabric for short durations. Checkpointing saves each GPU's model state, ranging from 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU, for recovery and debugging. We aim to minimize GPU pause time during checkpointing and increase checkpoint frequency to reduce the amount of lost work after a recovery." (accessed 2026-10-30)
- Use in dive: mental model. Checkpoint traffic is a burst, not a stream: the storage is sized for peak write bandwidth during a few seconds, and the GPUs wait unless the save is asynchronous (group D). Cross-check [derived]: 405.85e9 parameters x 14 bytes (weights plus optimizer, no gradients; section 3, W2) = 5.68 TB; spread over 16,384 GPUs that is 347 MB per GPU on average, so the 4 GB maximum reflects uneven sharding across ranks or extra state, not the average (the paper does not break it down). At the fabric's 2 TB/s sustained, 5.68 TB is 2.8 s of write.

**T04-7. Meta's study of its two research clusters (RSC-1, 16K A100 GPUs; RSC-2, 8K; 11 months, 4 million jobs, over 150 million A100 GPU-hours) measured job mean time to failure (MTTF) falling inversely with job size: 47.7 days for 8-GPU jobs, 7.9 hours for 1,024-GPU jobs, about 10 hours at 4K GPUs. **T04-9. Meta defines Effective Training Time Ratio (ETTR) as productive runtime over wallclock time of a job run and derives, for long high-priority runs, E[ETTR] ~= (1 - N_nodes r_f (u_0 + dt_cp/2)) / (1 + w_cp/dt_cp), with the Daly-Young optimal interval dt_cp* = sqrt(2 w_cp / (N_nodes r_f)). Here w_cp is the time a checkpoint write costs the job, u_0 the restart overhead and dt_cp the interval. The approximation is within about 5% of a Monte Carlo estimate.**
- Location: 2410.21680v2, Sec II-D (ETTR definition), Sec III "Approximating E[ETTR] analytically", Equations 1 to 3; Appendix A.
- Verdict: NEW
- Evidence: https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21680v2 | Tier 3, preprint, first-party operator | formula | "ETTR is defined as the ratio of productive runtime to available wallclock time of a job run." "We consider the intervals between checkpoints as dt_cp with each checkpoint write incurring a constant time penalty of w_cp, the time it takes to perform initialization tasks (e.g., loading checkpoints, etc.) as u_0 ... The MTTF for the job is (N_nodes r_f)^-1." Eq. 2: "E[ETTR] ~= (1 - N_nodes r_f (u_0 + dt_cp/2)) / (1 + w_cp/dt_cp)". "a classic result by Daly and Young [23, 63, 17, 16] shows that, under some limiting assumptions, the optimal checkpointing interval is approximately dt_cp* = sqrt(2 w_cp / (N_nodes r_f))". "Comparing to a Monte Carlo approach ... even for large, long-running hypothetical jobs (e.g., 8k GPUs), we find that the approximation above is accurate to within ~5%." Appendix A assumptions: "dt >> u_0, w_cp, q and R >> u_0, q, dt_cp, w_cp". (accessed 2026-10-30)
- Use in dive: the formula behind the checkpoint-interval calculator. Mental model: each failure costs a restart plus, on average, half an interval of lost work; each checkpoint costs its blocking time; the optimum balances the two and scales as the square root of (checkpoint cost x MTTF).

Meta's measured 7.9 h for 1,024-GPU jobs is shorter than even the 17.5 row at 128 nodes (10.7 h); the paper does not reconcile the two, and the counting rules differ (r_f counts NODE_FAIL plus health-check-attributed failures on jobs above 128 GPUs, T04-8, while the Figure 7 MTTF is per job-size bucket) [our reading]. Use the table as an order-of-magnitude planning aid, not a prediction. Llama 3's observed 3.1 h between unexpected interruptions matches the 3.79 row by construction.

Reading: the reference 256-GPU job sees a hardware interruption every 2 to 13 days, the 16K-GPU job every 1 to 5 hours. Same hardware, same rate per node; only the multiplier changes.

### W2. Checkpoint bytes and the write bandwidth a target save time needs

**What is saved.** Weights plus optimizer state, not gradients: gradients are recomputed every step, and production state lists name parameters, FP32 replica, momentum and variance (T04-35). With BF16 weights (2 bytes), FP32 master (4) and Adam moments (4 + 4), that is 14 bytes per parameter; 12 if the FP32 master is the only weight copy saved [our reading, from T01-9's components]. The 16 bytes of T01-9 is the upper bound. Data-loader, RNG, scheduler and step state are kilobytes to megabytes (T04-23, T04-35).

| Model | Parameters (T01-3) | 12 B/param | 14 B/param | 16 B/param (upper bound) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Llama 3.1 8B | 8,030,261,248 | 96.4 GB | 112.4 GB | 128.5 GB |
| Llama 3.1 70B | 70,553,706,496 | 846.6 GB | 987.8 GB | 1,128.9 GB |
| Llama 3.1 405B | 405,853,388,800 | 4.87 TB | 5.68 TB | 6.49 TB |

70B at 14 B/param on 256 GPUs: 3.86 GB per GPU, 30.9 GB per p5.48xlarge. Cross-checks: Llama 3 reported 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU (T04-5); OPT-175B wrote 1.6 TB in 992 files of about 1.7 GB (T04-18); ByteDance's 175B save took 200 s, about 12 to 14 GB/s end to end (T04-35).

**Synchronous save: aggregate write bandwidth = checkpoint bytes / target blocking time.** For the 70B checkpoint (987.8 GB):

| Target blocking time | Aggregate write | Per p5.48xlarge | FSx for Lustre SSD capacity at the documented disk throughput (T04-29) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 300 s (Meta's assumed w_cp, T04-9) | 3.3 GB/s | 0.82 Gbps | 3.3 TiB at PERSISTENT-1000; 26 TiB at PERSISTENT-125 |
| 60 s | 16.5 GB/s | 4.1 Gbps | 16.5 TiB at PERSISTENT-1000; 33 TiB at PERSISTENT-500; 132 TiB at PERSISTENT-125 |
| 10 s (Meta's 12K-GPU target, T04-11) | 98.8 GB/s | 24.7 Gbps | 99 TiB at PERSISTENT-1000; 790 TiB at PERSISTENT-125 |

Method: TiB needed = (GB/s x 1,000) / (MBps per TiB), treating AWS's MBps as 10^6 bytes per second (U04-7). Writes run at the lower of network and disk throughput (T04-29), so the disk column governs. Per-client ceilings (100 Gbps without EFA, 700 Gbps with, T04-30) are far above the per-node need in every row: the file system's aggregate throughput, bought by provisioning capacity, is the constraint. Holding the default 10 retained checkpoints (TorchTitan, T04-23) needs about 9 TiB, so for a 60 s or faster target, bandwidth rather than capacity sets the file-system size.

**S3 request rate.** With a multipart part size P, one 70B checkpoint is 987.8 GB / P PUT requests: about 123,000 at 8 MB, 15,400 at 64 MB. At the documented floor of 3,500 PUT/s per partitioned prefix (T04-27), 8 MB parts on one prefix take at least 35 s of request rate alone. The connector's part size was not verified (U04-8); the arithmetic shows why prefix strategies exist (T04-26) and why S3 Express One Zone's 100,000 to 200,000 writes per second per directory bucket (T04-28) changes the picture.

**Asynchronous save.** Only the device-to-host copy blocks: 3.86 GB per GPU, "several seconds" in MegaScale's pinned-memory implementation (T04-14), overlapped with the next forward and backward in TorchTitan's pinned mode (T04-22). The background write must still finish before the next checkpoint starts (T04-22's `maybe_wait_for_saving`), so the storage bandwidth requirement becomes bytes / interval rather than bytes / blocking time: 987.8 GB every 48 minutes (W3) is 0.34 GB/s.

**Host memory for an in-memory tier.** 30.9 GB per node against 2,048 GiB of host memory on p5.48xlarge (T05-2) is 1.4% per copy; HyperPod's minimum 20% allocation (T04-31) holds about 14 copies.

### W3. Optimal checkpoint interval and expected ETTR for the 70B run

Formulas from T04-9: dt* = sqrt(2 w_cp / (N_nodes r_f)); E[ETTR] ~= (1 - N_nodes r_f (u_0 + dt/2)) / (1 + w_cp / dt). N_nodes = 32, r_f = 6.50 per thousand node-days (T04-8), so N r_f = 0.208 failures per day (MTTF 4.8 days, W1). Restart overhead u_0 = 10 min (inside Meta's 5 to 20 min, T04-11; MegaScale's detect-diagnose-reload budget is 10 + 15 min, T04-13).

| Blocking checkpoint cost w_cp | dt* | dt* in 17.5 s steps | E[ETTR] |
|---|---|---|---|
| 300 s (synchronous, slow storage) | 4.4 h | 902 | 0.961 |
| 60 s (synchronous, W2 60 s row) | 2.0 h | 403 | 0.982 |
| 10 s | 48 min | 165 | 0.992 |
| 2 s (asynchronous, staging only) | 21.5 min | 74 | 0.996 |

At r_f = 17.5 (a bad month), w_cp = 60 s gives dt* = 1.2 h and E[ETTR] = 0.969; at r_f = 2.34, dt* = 3.3 h and 0.989.

The same formulas at 16,384 GPUs (2,048 nodes, r_f = 6.50, MTTF 1.8 h):

| w_cp | u_0 | dt* | E[ETTR] |
|---|---|---|---|
| 300 s | 10 min | 33 min | 0.66 |
| 60 s | 10 min | 15 min | 0.79 |
| 10 s | 10 min | 6 min | 0.86 |
| 2 s | 5 min | 2.7 min | 0.93 |

Reading [derived, our reading for the interpretation]: at 256 GPUs failures cost 1 to 4% whatever reasonable checkpoint design is used, and the practical gain from asynchronous saves is a shorter interval (less lost work), not a different order of goodput. At 16K GPUs the restart overhead u_0 dominates: with w_cp near zero, a 10-minute restart alone caps ETTR at 1 - (2,048 x 6.5e-3 / 86,400 s) x 600 s = 0.91. That is why Meta and MegaScale invest in restart time (T04-11, T04-13) and why peer-recovery designs that avoid a job restart exist (T04-42, T06-14). Note the formula's validity condition (u_0 + dt/2 much smaller than MTTF, T04-9) weakens in the 16K rows.

**Step-based configs.** TorchTitan's default interval of 500 steps (T04-23) is 2.4 h at 17.5 s per step: near-optimal for a 60 s synchronous save on this job, and about three times too long for an asynchronous one. Set the interval in steps from dt* / step time.

### W4. What Llama 3's 90% says about its recovery budget

419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days is a rate of 8.98e-5 per second (T04-1). If interruptions were the only loss, ETTR >= 0.90 requires the average lost time per interruption (restart plus half an interval) to be at most 0.10 / 8.98e-5 s = 1,113 s, about 18.6 minutes, and less once planned interruptions and checkpoint costs are included. Meta states it reduced "job startup and checkpointing time" to get there (2407.21783v3 Sec 3.3.4).

### W5. Data loading: bytes per second per GPU for text pre-training

Tokens per second per GPU = MFU x peak / (6N), ignoring attention FLOPs (T01-14, T01-18). H100 BF16 dense peak 989.5 TFLOPS (T05-14, vendor specification); MFU 40% (register 02 working assumption). Bytes per token on disk: 4 for Llama 3's 128,256 vocabulary in Megatron's format (T04-50).

| Model, cluster | Tokens/s per GPU | Bytes/s per GPU | Per p5.48xlarge | Whole job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 70B, 256 H100 | 935 | 3.7 KB/s | 30 KB/s | 0.96 MB/s (239K tokens/s; matches 4.19M tokens / 17.5 s) |
| 8B, 256 H100 | 8,215 | 33 KB/s | 263 KB/s | 8.4 MB/s |
| 405B as run by Meta (400 TFLOPs/GPU on 16,384 GPUs, T01-6) | 164 | 0.66 KB/s | 5.3 KB/s | 10.8 MB/s (2.69M tokens/s) |

Reading [our reading, from the arithmetic]: for text LLM pre-training, steady-state token reading is not a storage problem. The 405B job read about 11 MB/s from a fabric sustaining 2 TB/s (T04-5), five millionths of it; the same fabric writes the 405B checkpoint (5.68 TB, W2) in 2.8 s. Storage is the bottleneck at four moments instead: the checkpoint save burst (W2), the restart read of checkpoint and data indices (T04-14, T04-18, T04-45), start-up metadata storms when thousands of ranks list or open objects (T04-51's eager listing, T04-27's request rates), and data whose bytes per sample are orders of magnitude larger than text tokens (images, video), which this dive does not cover. Size training storage for checkpoints and restarts; a pre-tokenised, memory-mapped text corpus is served from page cache or local NVMe after the first read.

## 4. Mental models worth teaching

1. **A synchronous job is a series system.** It fails when any node fails, so its MTTF is the node MTTF divided by the node count: 13 days at 256 GPUs becomes under 2 hours at 16K (T04-7, W1). Every reliability technique in this area exists because of that division.
2. **Goodput loss per failure = restart time + half an interval of lost work; per checkpoint = its blocking time.** The optimal interval balances the two and scales as sqrt(checkpoint cost x MTTF) (T04-9, W3). At small scale checkpoint cost dominates the design; at large scale restart time does (W3).
3. **About 90% is what the best operators reach at 10K+ GPUs, with heavy automation.** Meta (Llama 3, more than 90%), ByteDance (MegaScale, over 90%) and Meta's research clusters (0.85 to 0.9) agree (T04-1, T04-13, T04-11). Vendor percentages without conditions do not belong next to these (T04-52).
4. **Save is asynchronous; load is not.** A checkpoint can hide behind training once the device-to-host copy is done, but nothing runs until a restart has read its state back (T04-14, T04-20). The optimizer step is the fence for the copy (T04-22).
5. **Memory is a storage tier if a peer holds a copy.** Host RAM plus one replica on another node recovers single-node failures at memory speed; object storage is the backstop (T04-33, T04-31, T04-39). Data-parallel replicas are the same idea taken further: the state already exists on healthy peers (T04-42, T06-14).
6. **A checkpoint is more than weights.** Optimizer moments are most of the bytes; data-loader position, RNG, scheduler and step are a few kilobytes but decide whether the resumed run is the same run (T04-23, T04-35, T04-44, T04-45).
7. **Data order should be a function, not a history.** Precompute the permutation from a seed and checkpoint only a counter, and resumption is instant and survives a change of world size (T04-45, T04-46).
8. **Slowness costs as much as failure and hides better.** 10.4% of GPU-hours lost to stragglers at ByteDance, mostly from software and workload imbalance rather than bad machines (T04-47, T04-48); detection is per-rank timing of compute, because the waiting shows up in collectives (T04-15, T04-6).
9. **Text tokens are cheap; checkpoints are not.** A 70B job on 256 H100 reads about 1 MB/s of tokens and writes about 1 TB per checkpoint (W2, W5). Size storage for the checkpoint burst and the restart read.

## 5. Contradictions

**C04-1. Llama 3 Table 5, count versus percentage.** "Faulty GPU 148 30.1%": the counts sum to 419 and the other rows' percentages use 419 as the denominator, so 148 should read 35.3% (or the count should be about 126). The text's "GPU issues ... 58.7%" sums the printed GPU percentages; from counts it is 64.0%. Both sides: 2407.21783v3 Table 5 and Sec 3.3.4 text (T04-3). Unresolved (U04-1).

**C04-2. PyTorch `StagingOptions` defaults versus `async_save`'s own stager.** `StagingOptions` documents and defaults all four options (pinned memory, shared memory, async staging, non-blocking copy) to True; `async_save` without a stager constructs `StagingOptions(False, False, False, False)`. Both in pytorch v2.15.0 (T04-21). Code is the authority on behaviour: the bare call stages synchronously into pageable memory.

**C04-3. AWS tiered-checkpointing example versus PyTorch.** The AWS setup page initialises `backend="nccl"` and calls `async_save`; PyTorch v2.4.0, v2.7.1 and v2.15.0 reject async save without a CPU backend (T04-32). Code reading says the example fails as written; not tested, and the HyperPod library was not read (U04-4).

**C04-4. Megatron `async_save` docstring versus validation.** The field docstring says async save works "only with `torch_dist`"; `__post_init__` accepts `torch_dcp` and `fsdp_dtensor` with NVIDIA Resiliency Extension; `arguments.py` disables async save without `--use-persistent-ckpt-worker` (T04-38). All in core_v0.20.0; the validation code governs.

**C04-5. AWS goodput claim versus first-party operator reports.** AWS's HyperPod page states "60-80% typical goodput on self-managed clusters" (T04-52, vendor claim, no conditions); Meta and ByteDance report about 90% on their self-managed clusters at 10K+ GPUs (T04-1, T04-11, T04-13). Not a strict contradiction (different populations: hyperscale operators with in-house automation against typical customers), but the dive must not set them side by side without that qualifier.

**C04-6. OPT-175B GPU count.** The final note states "~33 days of continuous training on 1024 80GB A100s"; the checkpoint was "992 files" (T04-17, T04-18). [our reading] 992 GPUs ran the job with the rest as spare capacity; the notes read here do not say.

## 7. Reading path for a newcomer

1. **Llama 3 paper, Sec 3.3.4** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783v3). Two pages that set the stakes: 419 interruptions in 54 days, the cause table, 90% effective training time, stragglers and power swings (T04-1 to T04-6).
2. **Meta's "Revisiting Reliability" paper, Sec III and Appendix A** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21680v2). Turns the anecdote into a model: MTTF = 1/(N r_f), ETTR, the Daly-Young interval and the 10-second target (T04-7 to T04-12). Read after Llama 3 so the formulas have a case to explain.
3. **MegaScale Sec 4.4 and 6.3** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15627v1). A second operator's recovery pipeline: heartbeat, diagnose, two-stage checkpoint, broadcast on load, stragglers (T04-13 to T04-16).
4. **OPT-175B chronicles** (metaseq at the pinned SHA). The same problems before the tooling existed; short and concrete (T04-17, T04-18).
5. **PyTorch `torch/distributed/checkpoint/state_dict_saver.py` and `staging.py` at v2.15.0**, then **TorchTitan's `checkpointer/dcp.py`** at v0.4.0. How asynchronous saving actually works and where the fence is (T04-19 to T04-24).
6. **HyperPod managed tiered checkpointing setup page** and the **s3torchconnector README** at v1.5.0. The AWS paths, read with C04-3 in mind (T04-25 to T04-32).
7. **ByteCheckpoint Sec 2** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20143v4). The full list of what a checkpoint holds and why resharding is routine (T04-35, T04-36).
8. **TorchFT README** at v0.2.0. Recovery without a restart, once the checkpoint model is clear (T04-42).
9. **Megatron `gpt_dataset.py` and `data_samplers.py`** at core_v0.20.0, and the **torchdata StatefulDataLoader README**. Deterministic data order and resumable loaders (T04-44, T04-45).
10. **ByteDance straggler study** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05713v2). Last, because it reframes slowness as a software problem once failures are understood (T04-47, T04-48).
