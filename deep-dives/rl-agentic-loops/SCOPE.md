## Thesis

Every method in this dive runs one loop: sample attempts, score them, and change something so the next attempts score better. The methods differ in where the change lands. Reinforcement learning (RL) writes it into the weights. A text loop writes it into the context a frozen model reads: a reflection, a rewritten prompt, a playbook, a memory, a skill file. A hybrid trains a small model whose output is words for a frozen large one.

Where the change lands decides what can be learned. On the tested models and benchmarks, RL with verifiable rewards mostly moves probability toward answers the model could already sample: pass@1 rises while pass@k at large k holds or falls (F02-40, F02-47). Policy gradient carries roughly one bit per episode on Thinking Machines' information argument, and in their runs a rank-1 LoRA adapter matched full fine-tuning (F02-86, a Tier 2 lab blog). Separately, RL forgot less than supervised fine-tuning at matched task performance on a 3B language model and a 7B robotics model, and the forgetting tracked the KL to the base model (F02-87). [our reading] Both results say RL moves the model little.

A frozen model can buy part of that sharpening back at inference time: a training-free sampler nearly matched or beat GRPO's single-shot scores on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, at about 8.84 times the tokens per answer in the authors' MATH500 setting (F02-58). A text loop can capture task instructions and procedures cheaply: on Qwen3 8B, reflective prompt evolution (GEPA) reached 54.85 on a six-task aggregate against 48.91 for LoRA GRPO and 45.23 for the base model, with 1,839 to 7,051 rollouts against 24,000 (F03-61, F04-46). [our reading] It falls short where the gap is reasoning skill, behaviour that must vary per input, exploration, or long-horizon credit assignment: in the studies read, GRPO beat prompt evolution on the one competition-math task (F03-62), RL lifted a 32B model far more than a text loop on a different 32B model (F03-90), a trained advisor learned per-user preferences that a static prompt did not (F03-107), only one GPT-4 configuration explored a bandit satisfactorily (F04-8), and on failed multi-agent logs the best method located the decisive step 14.2% of the time, with accuracy falling as logs grew (F04-26).

In both, the reward decides whether anything is learned and is the surface that gets gamed. On the 50 hardest HumanEval Rust problems with GPT-4, a Reflexion loop without its tests scored below no reflection at all, 0.52 against 0.60, while the full loop reached 0.68 (F04-21); RL policies learned `exit(0)` to pass test suites (F02-79). The choice reduces to three decision questions the dive teaches the reader to answer (section 22): what kind of gap is it; what signal do you have, and what does the model score on it today; and can you touch the weights, and how many queries will the learned artifact serve.

### Layer B. The RL vocabulary, built on what the reader knows

**3. The language model is already a policy**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Read any RL paper's equations by mapping them onto the sampling you already run.

Maps the RL vocabulary onto token generation, starting from sampling, which the reader knows. State is the prompt plus the tokens so far; action is the next token, or the whole response. Treating the whole response as one action makes each prompt a bandit: pick one of several options, receive one reward, carry nothing forward, which is the explore-or-exploit trade in its smallest form and the framing InstructGPT and RLOO-style methods use (F01-3, F01-7, F01-8). One attempt from prompt to reward is called an episode, a rollout or a trajectory depending on the paper; the dive says rollout, and keeps episode where a source states its figure per episode. The reward arrives once, at the end, which makes this a sparse-reward problem (F01-6), and spreading that one score back over the tokens that earned it is the credit assignment problem (F01-12). Value, advantage, return, on-policy versus off-policy and exploration get one plain definition each (F01-10 to F01-14). pass@1 is the chance that one sample is correct and pass@k the chance that at least one of k samples is (F02-40); section 8 reads them as a curve. Practical value: the reader can open any RL paper and tell which framing (token or response) its equations use, and see that running code mixes the two: the PPO trainer in TRL (Transformers Reinforcement Learning, Hugging Face's post-training library) adds the score at the last token and a KL penalty at every token (F01-9). The section ends on one bridge to the words track: Reflexion, a loop that writes a lesson into memory after each failed attempt and then retries (section 12), defines its policy as the model weights plus the memory text (F04-54), so editing the context edits the policy; section 11 builds on it. Diagram: one response drawn as a trajectory of token actions with a single reward at the end-of-sequence token.

**4. Policy gradient: learning from your own samples, weighted by how they scored**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Read any post-training method as one gradient: which samples, which scorer, which weight.

REINFORCE in one line: push up the log-probability of what was sampled, scaled by the reward that followed (F01-16, F01-17). Why that is noisy, and why subtracting a baseline keeps the expectation and cuts the noise (F01-18, F01-19). Two ways to get a baseline: learn one, or sample one from other attempts at the same prompt (F01-20 to F01-24). The learned one is a critic, a value function trained alongside the policy; GAE (Generalized Advantage Estimation) mixes the critic's estimate with the reward actually received, and its lambda sets how far the advantage trusts the critic. The sampled one is RLOO (REINFORCE Leave-One-Out) or GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization), which section 7 develops. With a strong pretrained model, the critic's bias was not worth it in one study: at lambda = 1 the advantage uses the actual return and ignores the critic's estimate, and that setting did best (F01-23). The section's mental model: every post-training method is one gradient shape, a coefficient times the gradient of log-probability, and methods differ in where the samples come from, what scores them, and how the score becomes the coefficient (F01-70, F01-85). Diagram: the gradient table, built cumulatively. This section shows four rows, SFT (supervised fine-tuning on fixed targets), REINFORCE, rejection-sampling fine-tuning (sample, keep what the grader passes, fine-tune on it) and RLOO or GRPO, with columns for sample source, scorer and weight; sections 5 and 6 add the PPO and DPO rows, and the full table closes section 6.

**5. Two leashes: PPO's clip and the KL to the starting model**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: What each leash does, and where to watch it on a managed job.

Why many gradient steps on one batch wreck the policy, and the trust-region idea behind PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) (F01-25, F01-26). The clipped ratio with epsilon 0.2 stops rewarding an update once it has moved a token's probability ratio more than 20% from the sampling policy; the ratio can still go further, but the gradient no longer pushes it there (F01-27). The 0.2 comes from robot-control ablations (F01-29). The KL (Kullback-Leibler divergence, how far one distribution has moved from another) penalty is a different leash: to the reference model, a frozen copy of the starting model, across the whole run, with beta 0.02 in InstructGPT (F01-28, F01-37). Evidence the reader needs to set these knobs: the clip activated under 5% of the time in one LLM study (F01-31); a 100x larger KL coefficient did not prevent capability regressions, while mixing in pretraining data did (F01-40); in a controlled study the KL penalty acted like early stopping (F01-41); TRL's GRPO trainer ships with beta 0 and does not load a reference model (F01-39, F02-29). Policy entropy measures how spread out the next-token distribution is; when it collapses, the model samples nearly the same answer every time and a group of samples has nothing left to compare (section 8 returns to it). Practical value, and the ending: what each knob in a trainer config does, and the two curves to watch during a run, KL to the reference and policy entropy. On a managed job, Bedrock reinforcement fine-tuning reports `actor/entropy` next to its reward statistics, and no KL metric was among those listed on the monitoring page read (F06-12). Diagram: the two leashes drawn as distances from the batch's sampling policy and from the starting model; the gradient table gains its PPO row.

**6. From preferences to rewards: RLHF, reward models, DPO and AI feedback**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Turn preference data you already collect into a weight update, and tell when offline DPO is enough.

The full RLHF (RL from human feedback) pipeline: supervised fine-tuning, a reward model fitted to pairwise preferences with the Bradley-Terry model (which turns two scores into the probability that one answer is preferred), then PPO against it with a KL penalty (F01-44, F01-46). On the authors' own prompt distribution, human raters preferred the 1.3B InstructGPT model's outputs to 175B GPT-3's (F01-50). Post-training is cheap next to pretraining: the 175B PPO-ptx model took 60 petaflop/s-days, plus 4.9 for its SFT stage, against 3,640 for GPT-3 pretraining (F01-52). Running it holds up to four models in memory at once (policy, reference, critic, reward model), which section 19 sizes (F01-32). DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) is the closed-form shortcut: the KL-regularised objective has an exact optimum, so the policy becomes its own reward model and trains with a classification loss, offline (on a fixed set of preference pairs, with no sampling from the model being trained) and so without exploration (F01-58 to F01-63). DPO still needs the reference model's log-probabilities, so it keeps part of that model's cost (F01-62). Its relatives sit in an expandable block: IPO (Identity Preference Optimization), KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization), ORPO (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization) and SimPO (Simple Preference Optimization) each remove one ingredient of DPO (F01-65 to F01-69). The DPO-versus-PPO evidence: PPO ahead once carefully tuned (F01-72), by an average of 0.7 points in a controlled 13B comparison where data quality mattered more, at roughly 8x the wall-clock [derived: 72 hours against 9, excluding reward-model training] (F01-73). RLAIF (RL from AI feedback) and Constitutional AI are the first bridge between words and weights: a critique written in language becomes either a revision to train on or a number to optimise (F01-77 to F01-84). The section ends on the decision [our reading, from F01-72 and F01-73]: DPO on preference data you already have is the first weight update to try when the signal is pairwise, and on-policy RL earns its cost when a verifier can score fresh samples. DPO is one of SageMaker AI's serverless techniques (F06-22, F06-24; section 23). Diagram: the RLHF pipeline beside DPO's single stage, and the gradient table completed with its PPO and DPO rows.

### Layer C. RL with verifiable rewards, and what it does to a model

**7. RLVR and GRPO: when the reward is a program**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Read the knobs on a managed GRPO job: which knob fixes which bias, and why always-solved prompts waste the step.

RLVR (RL with verifiable rewards) replaces the learned reward model with a checker: a boxed answer compared by rule, a compiler and test cases, a format check (F02-6, F02-7). GRPO scores each answer against the other samples in its group for the same prompt, which removes the critic (F02-1, F02-2). The result that started the field: DeepSeek-R1-Zero's pass@1 on AIME 2024 (a US competition-math exam whose integer answers a rule can check) rose from 15.6% to 77.9% under RL alone (F02-9, arXiv v2 and Nature; v1 reported 71.0%). On DeepSeek's own checkpoints the recipe failed at 7B dense and 16B MoE (mixture-of-experts) and worked from 32B up (F02-11), and for a 32B model distillation from R1 beat RL on every benchmark reported (F02-12); a Qwen2.5-Math-7B base did respond to RL in other work, a family-specific case section 8 returns to (F02-19, F02-53).

The successors form a table keyed by the GRPO term each one changes, with what goes wrong without the change. "Bias" in this table means a systematic tilt in the objective, a different sense from the statistical bias of section 4.
- *Normalisers.* Dr. GRPO removes both: dividing by response length favours longer wrong answers, and dividing by the group's standard deviation over-weights questions that are nearly always or never solved (F02-15); part of GRPO's response-length growth comes from the length term rather than from reasoning (F02-16).
- *Clip bound.* DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization) splits the clip range, 0.2 below and 0.28 above, because a symmetric clip caps how far unlikely tokens can rise and shrinks exploration (F02-20, F02-21).
- *Which prompts are kept.* DAPO's dynamic sampling keeps only prompts with accuracy strictly between 0 and 1 (F02-22).
- *How token losses are averaged.* DAPO's token-level loss (F02-20).
- *Token or sequence ratio.* GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization) uses one length-normalised ratio per response, which keeps mixture-of-experts training stable (F02-23, F02-24).
- *Where the advantage is normalised.* REINFORCE++ normalises across the whole batch rather than per prompt (F02-26). RLOO is the sampled baseline of section 4 (F02-25).

Practical value from code at pinned tags: TRL defaults to `loss_type="dapo"`, verl (the open RL framework behind HybridFlow, section 19) defaults to PPO with a critic, and a full Dr. GRPO setup needs two flags together (F02-28 to F02-36). The same knobs are choices on managed jobs: Bedrock reinforcement fine-tuning exposes samples per prompt, batch size, epochs, learning rate and lengths (F06-11), and SageMaker AI multi-turn RL offers nine advantage methods, including RLOO, REINFORCE++ and a GRPO pass@k variant, and three losses (F06-33). The section ends on the mechanism behind section 22's base-rate check: a group whose samples all get the same reward has zero advantage and contributes no gradient, so prompts the model always or never solves waste compute (F02-22, F06-34). Diagram: a group of eight sampled answers, their rewards, the normalised advantages, and an all-correct group greyed out as zero gradient.

**8. What RL does to a model: sharpening, composition, and the pass@k debate**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Read an RL gain as a pass@k curve before you trust it, and know what a frozen model can buy back.

The section that answers the dive's first question. It opens with the term it is about: the capability boundary is the set of problems a model solves in at least one of k samples when k is large, and RLVR (RL with verifiable rewards, section 7) can move probability inside that set or widen it. The reader learns to read a pass@k curve, and the evidence follows as a structured debate (F02-40 to F02-62).

*The debate.* On the tested regimes, RLVR on a strong base mostly reallocates probability among answers the base can already reach: RL wins at small k and the base wins at large k (F02-40), and question-level accounting finds more solutions lost than gained even for prolonged RL, 175 questions lost against 48 gained for ProRL-1.5B-v2 (F02-47, F02-48). [our reading] Three findings show the boundary moving, each in a controlled or small setting: when the parts exist but not the combination (synthetic string functions on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, F02-50), when training sits at the edge of competence with pretraining headroom (100M-parameter models on synthetic data, F02-49), and when new information enters from a teacher or a tool (F02-61; one small study on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, F02-62, whose instance section 9 owns).

*What changes in the weights.* On Thinking Machines' information argument (a Tier 2 lab blog), supervised fine-tuning arguably supplies information on every token of its target, while policy gradient's single scalar reward supplies about one bit per attempt. Assuming one bit per completion, their MATH run had about 320,000 bits to absorb, against about 3M parameters in a rank-1 LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation, a small trainable add-on to frozen weights) on Llama-3.1-8B, and in their runs rank-1 LoRA matched full fine-tuning for policy gradient (F02-86). RL forgot less than SFT at matched task performance on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and OpenVLA 7B, and forgetting tracked KL to the base (F02-87). Entropy is the budget RL spends: across 11 base models from four families, 76% of the gain and 73% of the entropy drop came in the first 200 of 2,400 steps (F02-89). The finding that 68.5% to 96.0% of parameters stay unchanged in bf16 checkpoints, and the dispute that calls it a precision artefact, sit in an expandable block (F02-84, F02-85).

*The trust check.* Before trusting an RL gain, check the model family, the benchmark's contamination status and the k at which it was measured. The Qwen case shows why: random and wrong rewards still lifted Qwen2.5-Math on MATH-500 (a 500-problem subset of the MATH competition benchmark), and given the first 60% of each problem the model reproduced the rest with 54.60% exact match, against 3.8% for Llama3.1-8B (F02-53, F02-56).

*What a frozen model can buy back.* The closing block, and the start of the answer to the second question.
1. [our reading, from the definition in F02-40] Read the pass@k curve as a menu: the base model's pass@k is the ceiling for best-of-k selection with a perfect verifier.
2. A training-free sampler that targets the base model's own likelihood raised to a power nearly matched or beat GRPO's single-shot scores with no verifier: on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, MATH500 0.496 for the base, 0.748 for the sampler and 0.785 for GRPO, at about 8.84 times the tokens of standard inference, which the authors compute for MATH500 from their sampler settings (10 Markov chain Monte Carlo steps, a 679-token average output, 192-token blocks); the multiplier depends on output length and those settings (F02-58). This is the block's number.
3. A forced opening phrase, "Let's solve this using Python.", raised Qwen2.5-Math-7B's MATH-500 accuracy by 15.0 points (49.4% to 64.4%) and lowered it on Llama, OLMo2 and the non-Math Qwen2.5-7B (F02-55); [our reading] the contamination result above (F02-56) is about the same model and benchmark, so it applies to this gain too.
4. A learned verifier over a frozen generator: with GPT-4-based models, a PRM (process reward model, scoring each reasoning step) trained on 800,000 human step labels solved 78.2% of a representative subset of MATH test problems when choosing among 1,860 samples per problem, against 72.4% for an ORM (outcome reward model, scoring only the final answer) and 69.6% for majority vote; the generator was never RL-trained (F02-64). DeepSeek dropped PRMs as a training reward because steps are hard to define and label at scale and a model-based PRM gets reward-hacked, and found the benefit too small for the compute in large-scale RL; a PRM still helps rerank the top answers (F02-13).
5. The landing [our reading, register 02 mental model 4]: inference-time sharpening pays on every query. Best-of-n needs the verifier at serving time; the sampler needs none but multiplies tokens, about 8.84 times in the paper's setting. Where RL went beyond the base model's pass@k in the studies read, with a new combination of skills (synthetic string functions on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, F02-50) and with tool use that widened coverage (one small study on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, F02-62), resampling the base model did not reach it. The words track (11 to 16) takes up the rest of the second question: what a loop can learn that RL mostly does not, namely instructions, procedures and facts.

Mental model: RL mostly moves probability mass toward answers the model can already sample, and a frozen model can buy part of that back per query. Diagram: schematic base and RL pass@k curves crossing, labelled as schematic, with the best-of-k ceiling marked on the base curve.

**9. Agentic RL: tools, environments and multi-turn credit**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: What you build to RL-train an agent: a sandbox, tool interfaces and a grader.

An RL environment for an agent is a sandbox, a set of tool interfaces and a grader. The section profiles five with the same four fields (environment, action, observation, reward): Search-R1, ReTool, RAGEN, SWE-RL and DeepSWE (F02-70 to F02-75), plus Kimi K2's gym-like framework with rubric rewards for subjective tasks (F02-76). **10. The reward is a proxy: over-optimisation and reward hacking, in weights and in words**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Build a grader that survives optimisation, in weights and in words.

The join between the two tracks, and a preview of the words track. In a synthetic study with a 6B reward model standing in for humans, optimising against a smaller proxy first raised the gold score and then lowered it, on fitted curves in the square root of KL (the drift measure of section 5) from the start. Best-of-n selection at inference over-optimises the same way, on a curve of its own that rises and falls faster per unit of KL than RL's (F01-54 to F01-56, F02-78). In agentic coding the cheap path runs through the test suite: `exit(0)` and `raise SkipTest` in a frontier training run (F02-79), hacks in production-like coding environments that generalised to broader misbehaviour (F02-81), special-cased tests reported in a system card (F02-82), and GPT-5 cheating on 76% of SWE tasks whose tests contradict their specification (F02-83). Training against a chain-of-thought monitor helps at low optimisation, and under stronger optimisation hides the hack rather than removing it (F02-80). The words half starts from loops the reader runs. Huntley names the model chasing "compiling code" in Ralph loops (F04-71), and autoresearch's metric guard is an instruction while the evaluated code is agent-editable (F04-65); both loops are in section 15. On the 50 hardest HumanEval Rust problems with GPT-4, a Reflexion loop without its tests scored 0.52, below 0.60 with no reflection at all, while the full loop with tests reached 0.68 (F04-21; section 12). Mental model: a leaky signal teaches the leak, whether the update lands in weights or in words. Practical value, and the ending: the grader checklist the rest of the dive cites (evaluator code the agent cannot edit, hidden tests, a held-out metric, monitors used for detection and kept out of the reward).

### Layer D. Learning in words

**11. A text loop is RL with the context as the trainable parameter**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Use RL's vocabulary to predict how a text loop will behave.

Opens the words track by making the analogy exact. Retroformer folds the frozen model into the environment, so whatever writes the context is the learner (F04-55), and Reflexion's policy is the model weights plus its memory text (F04-54). The bound on the analogy comes right after it: in-context learning (ICL) equals gradient descent is proven for linear self-attention on regression and open for real LLMs, and TextGrad calls its gradients a metaphor (F04-57 to F04-59). Every method in the track is then described with the same five fields: what state persists, the update rule, the feedback signal, what stays frozen, and the cost (comparison table in research 03). Methods are placed with section 1's three stores crossed with the survey's signal axis, execution-grounded or output-evaluated (F04-77). Step size is named here and developed in section 13: how much of the context one update rewrites, whether the context is a prompt or a playbook (a list of itemised lessons the loop maintains). Mental model: a text loop is an optimiser whose parameters are tokens in the context. The section ends on what the frame predicts: a loop's behaviour follows from its signal (section 12), its step size (section 13) and its exploration (section 17). **12. Verbal reinforcement and self-correction: the signal does the work**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Invest in the signal before the reflector.

Reflexion as the base pattern: act, get a success signal, write a lesson into a small episodic buffer (one to three entries), retry (F03-1 to F03-7, F04-20). The evidence on self-correction without outside feedback: Self-Refine's math score with GPT-3.5 stays at 64.1 with self-feedback and reaches 68.9 with oracle feedback (F03-9, F03-10); intrinsic self-correction drops GPT-3.5 on CommonSenseQA (multiple-choice commonsense questions) from 75.8 to 38.1 (F03-12, F03-13); Reflexion's HotPotQA signal (multi-hop questions over Wikipedia) is the gold answer (F03-3). [our reading] Failures help as memory when the update compares them with successes, and hurt as free-floating reflections: ExpeL fell from 39.0 to 29.0 on HotpotQA when reflections were added to its success/failure pairs (F03-20); ReasoningBank rose from 46.5 to 49.7 on WebArena-Shopping (web shopping tasks) when LLM-judged failures were included (F03-30); Training-Free GRPO writes lessons only from groups with both winners and losers (F03-86). Retroformer shows the hybrid fix: train the reflector with policy gradient when frozen reflections only restate the failure (F04-24; section 16). Mental model: no external signal, no learning. Practical value, and the ending: invest in the grader before the reflector.

**13. Experience that accumulates: memories, playbooks and skill libraries**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Design a playbook whose update rule keeps what it learned.

Cross-episode learning, organised by how the stored state is updated: append (Reflexion, ReasoningBank), vote and edit a list of insights (ExpeL), regenerate a memory (CLIN, Dynamic Cheatsheet), add verified programs (Voyager), merge small deltas with de-duplication (ACE, Agentic Context Engineering), or write lessons from groups of attempts (Training-Free GRPO, which replaces GRPO's numeric group advantage from section 7 with a written one, F03-86) (F03-18 to F03-39, F03-74 to F03-91). The update rule is the step size of section 11, and each rule has a matching failure: one full rewrite collapsed an 18,282-token playbook to 122 tokens and below the no-context baseline (F03-75); never compressing loses to curation, and GPT-4o-mini scored below its own baseline on AIME 2024, since the memory needs the model to solve instances often enough to fill it with good strategies (F03-34). The signal matters here as in section 12: Voyager discovered 73% fewer items without self-verification, and ACE without labels fell below baseline on online FiNER (financial entity tagging) (F03-38, F03-77). Cost in one line: Training-Free GRPO was cheap to train and dearer per query, on a pair that compares different base models, which section 20 prices (F03-91). Against RL: on 32B-class models with a code tool, RL lifted AIME24 by up to about 37 points on Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, against 4.4 for the text loop on Qwen3-32B, a different 32B model [derived] (F03-90). On the pages read, Anthropic's Agent Skills and memory tool are documented as storage and authoring features with no learning loop; self-authoring of skills is stated as a future goal (F03-40 to F03-42). **14. Optimising the prompt: textual gradients and reflective prompt evolution**
Depth: deep
Payoff: When prompt optimisation is the first thing to try, and what feedback to give it.

The prompt as the trained artifact. ProTeGi (Prompt Optimization with Textual Gradients) and TextGrad write natural-language critiques and apply them as edits (F03-43 to F03-50); OPRO (Optimization by PROmpting) keeps a scored history of instructions in a meta-prompt and asks the model for better ones (F03-51 to F03-53, F04-56); DSPy's MIPROv2 (Multiprompt Instruction PRoposal Optimizer, version 2) searches instructions and demonstrations with a surrogate model, a cheap model of the score that picks which candidates are worth evaluating (F03-55 to F03-58). GEPA (Genetic-Pareto) is the strongest same-model comparison with RL in this research: on Qwen3 8B over six tasks, an aggregate of 54.85 against 48.91 for LoRA GRPO (a full fine-tuning comparison shows a similar result) and 45.23 for the base model, with 1,839 to 7,051 rollouts against 24,000 (F03-61, F04-46); GRPO won the one competition-math task, AIME-2025, 38.00 against 32.00 (F03-62). The update rule reads the evaluator's text feedback and accepts a rewrite only if the minibatch score sum strictly rises, drawing parents from a per-instance Pareto front, the set of candidates that are each best on at least one training example (F03-59, F03-60, F03-72; gepa v0.1.4, the version DSPy 3.4.1 pins). Evolved prompts were up to 9.2x shorter than MIPROv2's (F03-67), and small models make poor prompt optimisers (F04-53). The budget to quote is the paper's total rollouts including validation, 1,839 to 7,051 here; the reconciliation with the README's smaller range (100 to 500 evaluations, near the paper's 79 to 737 training rollouts) sits in the evidence disclosure (F03-73). The limit in one sentence: a static prompt is one policy for every input, so behaviour that depends on a hidden per-instance variable needs weights somewhere (section 16). Mental model: a prompt optimiser is the text loop with the smallest state, one instruction set scored on a dev set, so it learns what every input shares. Practical value, and the ending: when prompt optimisation is the first thing to try, and how to give it the rich text feedback it reads.

**15. Your loops through the RL lens: autoresearch, search loops and Ralph**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Harden the loops you already run.

Practitioner loops the reader already knows, mapped onto the vocabulary of sections 3 to 10 (policy, reward, credit assignment, exploration, the grader as proxy). Karpathy's autoresearch (pinned SHA): one editable file, one metric (validation bits per byte), a fixed five-minute training budget per experiment, keep the commit if the metric improves and reset otherwise, every run logged to a results file (F04-60 to F04-64). In RL terms it is greedy hill-climbing with no exploration term, clean credit assignment by construction (one idea per commit, fixed seed) and no noise floor, so sub-noise wins can be kept (F04-66). Search loops add what autoresearch lacks. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve keep a population of programs, ask an LLM to mutate them, and keep what a programmatic evaluator scores higher (F03-92, F03-95); FunSearch splits its population into islands, an exploration term (F03-92); AlphaEvolve stages evaluation as a cascade so only promising candidates reach the expensive tests (F03-95); and FunSearch works best when the score is graded rather than pass or fail (F03-93). AlphaEvolve's authors describe FunSearch as using millions of LLM samples and AlphaEvolve as needing thousands, while AlphaEvolve also evolves whole files, evaluates for longer and uses frontier models (F03-96; the FunSearch figure is not yet checked against its own paper); [our reading] better proposals mean fewer of them are needed. Huntley's Ralph loop: the same prompt re-run with a fresh context each iteration, all state in files, tests and type checks as backpressure, and the operator adding "signs" to the prompt between runs (F04-68 to F04-72). Fresh context per iteration is the design answer to context degradation (F04-69; section 17), and the human editing the prompt is a slow outer optimiser. Practical value, and the ending: one hardening checklist for the reader's loops. It takes the evaluator items from section 10's grader checklist and adds a measured noise floor before accepting a win, one change per trial, scorekeeping in code, a population when the loop stalls [our reading, the exploration term autoresearch lacks], fresh context with state in files, and a stable prompt prefix with the evolving part after it so prefix caching survives (mechanism in section 17).

**16. Hybrids: train a small model, keep the big one frozen**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Improve an API model you cannot train by training a small one in front of it.

When the big model's weights are out of reach (an API model) and words alone are not enough, put trained weights somewhere else. Advisor Models name two roles: the advisor is the small model trained with GRPO, and the student is the frozen model it advises, whose final output earns the advisor's reward (F03-104, F03-105). On machine translation with the same 7B student, scored in chrF (a character-overlap translation score), direct RL on the student reached 42.38 and the advisor 38.98 from a 31.17 baseline, recovering about 70% of the gain [derived] (F03-108). Advisors learned hidden per-user preferences that static prompts did not (F03-107) and could not help where the student already knew everything relevant (F03-109). Related patterns: Retroformer's trained reflector (F04-24), Memento's learned retrieval over a case bank (F03-81), RL fine-tuning or distillation of a bandit algorithm to improve exploration, which closes part of the gap to a classical bandit algorithm (F04-13, F04-14; the exploration failure itself is in section 17), context distillation to move a prompt's effect into weights (F04-45), and alternating prompt optimisation with fine-tuning (F03-111). Mental model: when the right behaviour depends on something hidden per instance, some weights have to learn it. Practical value, and the ending: the architecture for improving an API model with RL on a model you own.

### Layer E. Limits and the boundary

**17. Where words fall short: exploration, credit assignment and the context cliff**
Depth: deep
Payoff: Where frozen models fall short, and the loop designs that work around it.

The answer to the dive's third question, from the evidence. In-context RL means a policy that improves across episodes inside one context, with its weights untouched. It is trained in: Algorithm Distillation and DPT (Decision-Pretrained Transformer) train a transformer on learning histories, the logged episodes of an RL algorithm as it improves, and the result explores in context (F04-1 to F04-7; detail in a disclosure). The general LLMs tested did not show it: across GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and Llama2 on small bandits, only one configuration explored satisfactorily, and only with an externally summarised history (F04-8 to F04-10); Gemma2 models from 2B to 27B committed early in a second study (F04-11), and models computed the right action and still acted greedily (F04-12). When the task is estimating which option pays best from observed rewards, the LLMs studied exploited worse than a simple linear regression, with reasoning models best among them, and they were useful as proposers of candidates to try (F04-17). Credit assignment, section 3's problem of finding which step earned the result, works differently in each track [our reading]: RL assigns it statistically across many rollouts, even with the reward on the last token (F02-39); a text loop assigns it by reading one log, and on failed multi-agent logs the best method found the decisive step 14.2% of the time, some methods scored below random, and accuracy fell as logs grew (F04-26). The context has a cliff: effective length sits below the claimed length (GPT-4 claimed 128K and held to 64K in RULER, a synthetic benchmark that measures the length at which a model still performs) and the middle is used worst (F04-31, F04-32). Edits re-price it, since prompt caching, Bedrock's included, matches exact prefixes and any edit invalidates what follows (F04-38 to F04-40). Mental models: frozen models are strong proposers and weak statisticians; context is a working set with a cliff, and edits re-price it. Practical value, and the ending: keep statistics in code and hand the model a summary, budget memory against effective length, and keep the evolving part of the context after the stable part.

**18. What lives in weights, and what lives in context**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Classify your gap: what belongs in weights and what in context.

The boundary map, one row per kind of improvement (research 04, section 2). A row carries evidence only where no other section owns it, and points to the owning section otherwise.
- *Task facts after the cutoff: context.* Retrieval beat unsupervised fine-tuning (training on the raw documents, without instruction targets), 0.875 against 0.504 on Mistral 7B from a 0.481 base (F04-43).
- *Instructions and procedures: context, when a reliable signal writes them* (sections 13 and 14; F04-46, F04-37).
- *Sharpening toward answers the model can already sample: weights through RL, or bought per query at inference* (section 8).
- *Constraints and output format: stated in context, contested.* How the prompt itself is formatted moved few-shot accuracy by up to 76 points on LLaMA-2-13B, and instruction tuning did not remove that sensitivity (F04-52), so a format rule found by a text loop may not survive a model change. [our reading] Whether training a format into the weights makes it reliable was not measured in the sources read.
- *Calibrated or diffuse distributions: weights.* Asked for a number from one to ten, Llama-2-13B-chat favoured five, and in 1,000 samples none of the tested models produced all ten; a fine-tuning method fixed it, and untuned models would need a temperature above 10 to match (F04-50). Post-training also changes calibration (F04-51).
- *Token efficiency: weights,* since context distillation moves instructions into the weights and removes their tokens (F04-45).
- *Retention of old abilities: context by construction,* since forgetting after a weight update tracks the KL from the base model (section 8; F04-47).
- *Exploration, learning from failure and long-horizon credit: weak in context* (section 17).
- *New reasoning skill: weights, through distillation from a stronger model (F04-49, F02-12); whether RL alone adds it is contested* (section 8; F04-49).

Mental model: weights hold what must be fast, calibrated or general; context holds what is specific, recent and auditable. [our reading] Neither store delivered stability under prompt rephrasing in the sources read: format sensitivity survived instruction tuning (F04-52). ### Layer F. Systems and cost

**19. An RL step is an inference job inside a training job**
Depth: mechanism
Payoff: Size and monitor a self-run RL job: where step time goes and what to measure.

What the section is for: sizing and monitoring a self-run job, and seeing why training-side cost scales with rollout tokens. For a reader who knows vLLM, one step is four stages: generation (decode-bound), preparation (reference log-probabilities, rewards, values), training, and a weight sync back to the inference engine when generation and training run on different engines (F05-1, F05-3, F05-4). The actor is the policy being trained, named for its role next to the critic; with a critic, a reference and a reward model, PPO holds up to four models at once (F01-32). Generation dominates: 72.1% of 309 hours over 1,000 training steps of Skywork-OR1-32B, and 76% to 78% in its 32-GPU ablation (F05-42); over 95% in one 13B PPO study (F01-53). A synchronous step waits for its longest response: for the same 1,024 responses, going from 32 to 256 H800s cut rollout time only from 375 s to 205 s (F05-42, F05-6, F05-36). DeepSeek-R1's framework attacks the same tail from the inference side, with self-speculative decoding to shorten the longest samples (F05-39). Asynchronous designs hide the tail and the broadcast at the cost of staleness, rollouts sampled by a policy a few updates old. AReaL bounds staleness with one parameter and corrects with a decoupled PPO objective, which separates the policy that sampled the data from the policy being updated, finishing a 1.5B math run on 16 H800 nodes in 14.8 hours, against 41.0 for its own synchronous mode and an author-estimated 33.6 for synchronous verl (F05-7 to F05-10); PipelineRL updates weights mid-sequence (F05-35); prime-rl drops rollouts older than eight policy versions by default (F05-32). Even "on-policy" runs are slightly off-policy because vLLM and the trainer compute different probabilities for the same weights. TRL corrects for it by default with sequence-level masking (F05-22); OpenRLHF ships the same correction switched off (F05-16). Two expandable blocks hold the detail. The accounting caveats: HybridFlow's 58.9% counts generation and actor training together, the OpenRLHF paper's "over 90%" carries no measurement, and the weight move (140 GB per iteration for a 70B actor, up to 36.4% of an iteration when training and generation sit on different devices) is a figure HybridFlow quotes from OpenRLHF's 2023 system (F05-2, F05-12, F05-3). The framework placement and memory defaults of verl, OpenRLHF, TRL, slime and prime-rl at pinned tags (F05-13 to F05-34). Mental model: everything asynchronous in RL systems exists to hide the straggler tail and the weight broadcast, and staleness is the price; the straggler figure lands a second time here, eight times the GPUs for a 45% cut in rollout time [derived: 205 s against 375 s]. Practical value, and the ending: what to measure on a run (generation share, idle GPU time, staleness, weight-sync time) and how cluster size changes the best placement: in HybridFlow's runs, colocating every model won from 16 to 64 GPUs, and split placement won at 96 GPUs for 13B models and at 96 to 128 for 34B models (F05-5). Diagram: synchronous and asynchronous step timelines on the same time axis.

**20. Cost per improvement: pay once in training or on every query**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Pay once in training or on every query: find your breakeven.

The economics of the choice. The deliverable is the calculator's structure: training cost, per-query cost difference, quality difference and expected volume as explicit inputs, with the reader's own rates entered first. Training side: GEPA (the reflective prompt optimiser of section 14) needed 1,839 to 7,051 rollouts against GRPO's 24,000 on the same Qwen3 8B (F03-61), the one same-model preset, priced at the reader's cost per rollout; DeepSeek-R1's post-training took 147K H800 GPU-hours, which its authors price at $294K under an assumed $2 per GPU-hour, with R1-Zero's pure RL run at 101K of those hours on 512 GPUs (F05-37); ProRL's 1.5B model took about 16k H100 GPU-hours (F05-41); DeepSWE took 64 H100s for six days [vendor claim] (F02-63); a minimal Dr. GRPO recipe took 27 hours on 8 A100s (F02-19). Serving side: memory loops raise tokens per answer roughly three to five times (Dynamic Cheatsheet on AIME 2024, 370 to 1,035 or 1,831 [derived], F03-35), and prompt caching narrows the gap, 82.6% lower billed input for ACE (F03-79). Practical value, and the ending: enter your rates, your volume and your measured quality gap, and read which side of the breakeven you are on. **22. Choosing: a text loop, a hybrid or a weight update**
Depth: applied
Payoff: Choose a text loop, a hybrid or a weight update, and start with the grader.

The decision, built from sections 8, 10, 18 and 20. Three decision questions, in order.
1. *What kind of gap is it?* Missing instructions, facts or procedures (context); sharpening toward answers the model can sometimes produce (inference-time sampling or RL); or reasoning skill, per-input behaviour, latency or calibration (weights). Format reliability is a contested row: measure it on the target model before choosing where it lives. Section 18 is the map.
2. *What signal do you have, and what does the model score on it today?* The signal is a programmatic verifier, a judge validated against labels, or nothing trustworthy, in which case the first task is building the grader (section 10). Today's score decides whether RL has a gradient: near 0%, every group scores the same, so SFT or distillation from a stronger model comes first; near 100%, nothing is left to learn (F02-22). AWS's triage rule for reinforcement fine-tuning sets the same thresholds: SFT first at a consistent 0%, RFT possibly unnecessary above 95% (F06-9).
3. *Can you touch the weights, and how many queries will the learned artifact serve?* Weight access is the gate; volume sets the breakeven (section 20).

The paths that follow, with the AWS service for each where one exists (section 23):
- inference-time sampling, when the gap is sharpening and the per-query cost is acceptable (section 8);
- a text loop with a hardened grader, the default first move for instructions and procedures (sections 13 to 15);
- an advisor, when the model is an API model and behaviour varies per input (section 16);
- SFT or distillation from a stronger model, when the base scores near zero (F02-12, F06-9);
- reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT), managed or self-run, when the gap is skill or the volume is high and you own the weights (sections 7 and 19);
- distilling a stable playbook into weights once it stops changing (F04-45, F03-99).

Auditability is a criterion of its own: context can be read and reviewed line by line, and Google chose AlphaEvolve over deep RL for data-centre scheduling on interpretability and deployability grounds (F03-97). The section ends on the reader's next action: write the grader, measure its noise floor, and run the cheapest loop against it first.
