## doc-A: "21. Reading a Machine: The Counters That Say Which Part Is Full"

Scores
- argument 8: the claim (four slot shares tell you what to buy) and a worked 400-slot example arrive by paragraph 2, a real diagnosis by paragraph 3; paragraph 4 drops into a tour of the closing card.
- depth 9: the formula, the validation test, width normalisation, the flush correction and its size, per-instance counter access, and a measured GPU kernel. This is level 400 and above.
- clarity 7: nearly every term is built before use, but "card" is never defined, one estimate comes before the formula that justifies it, and several paragraphs carry six core names and four numbers at once.
- sentences 7: many sentences are excellent ("A card whose shares sum to 100% has passed no test. A program with a known answer is a test."), but the dense ones run long, and the ending has a dangling "one".
- parity 8: the same standard as the best file in the batch, and heavier going.

Findings, most damaging first
1. "A PMU card puts both sides on one page for one workload on one machine, and four questions read it, each catching one misreading: what ran and how it was counted, since two cards compare only under the same formula; how much work per second, since a share is not a speed; where the slots went; and what lies behind the largest loss, which decides the part to buy." "Card" is a coined term the reader has never met, and it then runs through the subheadings ("Recompute any card's four shares", "an uncorrected card", "a CPU card comes from inside a virtual machine"). The sentence is also a preview of the last block (faults 1 and 4). Fix: define the card in one clause where the sample card first appears, and keep the four questions for the block that uses them.
2. "On the evidence it has, the loss is the execution units: in our reading a faster or wider core would help this workload and more memory bandwidth would not." The section defines core bound as including "each operation waits for the result of the one before, as in a chain of long floating-point operations". A wider core does nothing for a dependency chain, and the run "counted level 1 only", so "wider" is unsupported. The verdict is also firmer than the opening's "though one counter cannot settle it". Fix: "a faster core; whether a wider one helps needs the level-2 split this run did not record."
3. "79,584,976 x 4 / 19,922,115,591 = 1.6 points" comes before the reader is told why a share of cycles can be added to a share of slots. That reason ("Every cycle has the same S slots, so a share of cycles is also a share of slots") only appears a block later, in "Arm's corrected formula, term by term". The number comes before its formula. Fix: move the term-by-term block ahead of the estimate.
4. The EC2 and tooling parts drift into a reference manual, with trivial facts at the same weight as decisive ones. "Intel and AMD sort slots the same four ways under other event names. From Ice Lake on, Intel cores count slots in a fixed counter and hold the four ratios in a register of their own..." Then "(and only with certain operating systems and kernels)": which ones? That is vague exactly where the reader would act. Then "Since 19 November 2026, EC2 Detailed Monitoring can also publish one-minute GPU metrics with no agent inside the instance; until you have confirmed that it carries SM activity, take that figure from DCGM." This hands the reader a check the writer did not make, as a dated news item.
5. The GPU half stops before the configuration choice. "The kernel's name, cutlass_80_simt_sgemm_128x128_8x4_nn_align1, carries simt, the general floating-point path: the float32 multiply did not go through the tensor pipeline, so this busy kernel says nothing about the tensor cores." The reader is not told why a plain FP32 GEMM skips the tensor cores, or which setting or precision would route it there. "at 70.49% of the GPU's compute throughput and 50.47% of its memory throughput" is printed without saying what Nsight Compute's percentages are percentages of, so the reader cannot use them. The CPU half explains every number; this half reports them.
6. Header description: "(vendor files and docs read 2026-11-20; the sample card is our single run on a DGX Spark)". That is provenance bookkeeping in the header (fault 5). The same header also uses "four shares of a CPU core's slots" before the reader knows what a slot is.
7. "The sort comes from a 2014 Intel paper." This is a provenance sentence in the prose, and the citations beside it point at a cookbook and a README, not the paper. Cite it or cut it. "(Intel writes uOp)" is trivia in the same vein.
8. The slot is defined twice. The opening gives "Each chance is a slot: one place, in one cycle, to pass one micro-operation from the front end ... to the back end", and the next block gives "In hardware terms a slot is the resources needed to process one micro-operation". The toy core also switches from 4 slots to 8 without comment ("a cycle that moved 2 of its 8 slots").
9. Card caption: "The bottom block, also marked 1, finishes the first question with how the counters became percentages." When a caption has to explain why two blocks carry the same number, the figure's layout is wrong. Put "how it was counted" beside block 1.
10. Ending: "To read a machine for your own workload, as section 22, Our Measurements, does for three stand-in workloads on three core types, pick one that exposes the slot events (on EC2, a size with full support) and run the workload under perf stat..." "One" has no antecedent (the nearest nouns are "workloads" and "core types"; the intended one is "a machine"), and the cross-reference sits between the verb and its object. The last paragraph is the one the reader acts on, so it should be the cleanest.
11. The flush paragraph is the densest on the page: "An uncorrected card on the Neoverse V1 or V3, the Cortex-X925 or the Cortex-A725 therefore reads front-end bound too high and bad speculation too low, by the same amount, and its back-end and retiring shares match Arm's." It is followed by two estimates from two programs and two executions. The content is right and the consequence ("the correction changes which of the two looks larger") is the best line in the block, but it is buried at the end of the paragraph. Lead with it.

## doc-B: "Roofline and Arithmetic Intensity"

Scores
- argument 8: the header states a real, testable claim (the precision choice moves the ridge more than a generation does), the table proves it, and the paragraph after it adds the sharper point: the regime changes only where the FLOPS ratio is not 2x. The opening itself is provenance and a definition list.
- depth 6: the ridge numbers, a decode ceiling worked to 69 tokens/s and the batch at which decode reaches the ridge (~200) are good. There is no roofline equation, no achieved-versus-peak gap, no KV-cache byte count, no failure modes of the model, and the levers are named rather than explained.
- clarity 5: for the stated reader, decode and prefill, KV cache, GEMM, "dense" and 2:4 sparsity are used before they are built, or never built at all, and several acronyms get a name and no explanation.
- sentences 7: short and declarative, mostly consequential, with a few list sentences and one broken cross-reference.
- parity 7: clean but thin. It reads as a briefing for someone who already has the concepts.

Findings, most damaging first
1. Terms are used before they are built. The first figure and the paragraph under it ("Decode at batch 1 sits about 200 times below the ridge at either precision", "Prefill on a long prompt reuses each weight across thousands of tokens") come before the subsection that says what prefill and decode are. "KV (key-value) cache" is a name only. "dense" runs through the caption and every table, and is explained only near the end ("Sparse rates apply only to weights pruned to the 2:4 structured pattern"), where "2:4" itself is never explained. A level 100 to 200 reader is lost on the first screen.
2. The model the section is named for is never written down. The plot's axis is "Attainable TFLOPS", but the prose never gives attainable = min(peak FLOPS, intensity x bandwidth), and never says why the slope is the bandwidth. "Below the ridge a workload is memory-bound and extra FLOPS do nothing for it" is asserted, not derived. Intuition, then formula, then decomposition, is missing in exactly the place it matters.
3. "A precision step on one chip moves it 2x on H200 (206 to 412) and almost 9x on Rubin (182 at BF16 to 1,591 at NVFP4)." The H200 figure is one step (BF16 to FP8). The Rubin figure is two (BF16 to NVFP4). The like-for-like Rubin step is 182 to 795, 4.4x. The thesis survives, but this sentence, its main support, overstates it.
4. Opening: "The roofline model, published by Williams, Waterman and Patterson in Communications of the ACM in April 2009, T3 ACM answers one question about any workload on any chip". The first thing on the page is authors, journal and month (fault 5). Then "Three quantities." is a fragment that opens a definition list (fault 1).
5. Names stand in for mechanism. "Second, GQA (grouped-query attention), KV-cache quantization, FlashAttention-class fusion and speculative decoding speed up decode by cutting the bytes moved per useful FLOP." Four techniques, none explained, no number. "keeps intermediate tensors in SMEM (shared memory), TMEM (Tensor Memory) or SBUF (State Buffer)" gives three expansions that are names, not explanations. "CUTLASS, CuTe and Triton on NVIDIA and NKI (Neuron Kernel Interface) on Trainium emit fused kernels; NVIDIA Compilers and Kernel Tooling and AWS Compilers and Kernel Tooling cover them." is a list of tools with no consequence (fault 2).
6. "Faster HBM pulls the ridge left. Architectures that work out of on-chip memory change the denominator itself: ... T2 NVIDIA Cerebras Wafer-Scale, Groq, SambaNova and Dataflow and Compute-in-Memory cover each." The first sentence is a bare assertion with no figure. The architectures get no bandwidth number, so the reader cannot say how far left. The closing run of section titles ("and Dataflow and Compute-in-Memory") reads as a broken list.
7. "Tokens per second tracks HBM bandwidth almost linearly until another limit binds." Which limit? The 69 tokens/s ceiling then uses spec-sheet bandwidth with no word on how much of it real kernels reach. A reader who budgets on it will over-promise. The failure modes are missing.
8. "First, the spec sheet gives a ceiling on batch-1 decode speed: HBM bandwidth divided by the bytes read per token, the active parameter bytes plus the KV-cache bytes. A 70-billion-parameter dense model in FP8 reads about 70 GB per token". The KV term is introduced and then silently set to zero. Say it is weights only and that KV reads lower the ceiling further. Likewise "KV-cache capacity caps the batch, because each request's KV cache grows with context length" gives no bytes per token, so the reader cannot compute the cap.
9. "Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel to fill the KV (key-value) cache, so every weight read serves every prompt token in the batch." The "so" attaches the weight reuse to filling the cache. The reuse comes from processing the prompt's tokens together.
10. Ending: "Quantization and Precision covers the full ladder and the numerics that make FP8 and FP4 usable at model scale." The section's last words point elsewhere. The reader's action is in the sentence before it and should be the close.
11. Title "Roofline and Arithmetic Intensity" is a topic label. The argument lives only in the header description.

## doc-C: "20. Fifteen Workloads, Five Shapes"

Scores
- argument 6: the opening is strong (a method, then a worked 9.8 against 11.71 GB/s count with a pitfall by paragraph 2), but the section carries two theses (the part picks the machine, the shape picks how to rent it), and the headline one, "the first part to fill is often a CPU core", is undercut by the section's own count.
- depth 8: per-core bandwidth, GiB conversion, codec lists, EBS and EFA figures, the Batch retry rule and AWS_BATCH_JOB_ATTEMPT, and checkpoint arithmetic. Practical and specific.
- clarity 6: most terms are built, but "shape" is coined in the title and used undefined on the first screen. A 15 x 11 grid needs a six-word legend. Slot shares are compared across cores of different width.
- sentences 6: several garbled sentences, one that contradicts its own arithmetic, and a refrain of "(single runs)".
- parity 6: as much evidence as the best files, assembled as a catalogue, with slips a careful reader catches.

Findings, most damaging first
1. The thesis is undercut by the section's own count. The claim: "on a machine rented for its GPU, the first part to fill is often a CPU core, and the number that decides it is the instance type's cores per GPU." The only count in the section shows the opposite: "plain decoding then needs on the order of one such worker, about 2 of the 12 cores each GPU gets" and "Plain decoding leaves the GPU full". The CPU-first case rests on one vendor post (ACT's 2.8x) and on "Per-sample image transforms multiply the CPU's cost per item", which has no multiplier. The grid section then says "Three of the CPU rows have one cause: LeRobot's video is AV1, written and read by CPU code by default", which contradicts the earlier finding that transforms, not AV1 decode, filled the CPU in the ACT case. Fix: give a per-item transform cost and state the thesis with its condition (per-sample transforms on the CPU).
2. "The physics stand-in, which leans on both halves of the core, ran 57,240 / 14,507 = 3.9 times faster on the X925, with the newer MuJoCo, close to none of the three". The three factors given were 6.5, 3.8 and 2.4, and 3.9 is within 3% of the 3.8 bandwidth factor. The sentence states the opposite of its own numbers. The point to make is that a match with one factor proves nothing about which part is full.
3. Title "Fifteen Workloads, Five Shapes" is a counting title of the "One Robot, One Loop" kind. It says how many and nothing that is true, and "shape" is a coined term the reader meets undefined in the title, in the header ("tell from its shape") and on the first screen.
4. "They sort the fifteen into five shapes, a grouping of ours like the parts and the place of each job in them, and a scheduler acts on the shape". "a grouping of ours like the parts and the place of each job in them" is the owner's fault 5 almost word for word ("Counting them as five is our grouping"), and it barely parses. The same paragraph splits the section into two arguments, and the shapes half does not arrive until the last third of the section.
5. "did useful work in 43.2% of its slots on the Jetson's Cortex-A78AE and lost 31.0% to the front end and 21.5% to the back end. On the Spark's X925 it did useful work in 45.7%". Shares are set side by side across cores of different width, though the section itself tells the reader width matters (the A725 is "half as wide"). A share is a share of each core's own width, so 43.2% against 45.7% compares different denominators. The conclusion drawn is also confounded and near-empty: "(single runs; MuJoCo 3.17.0 on the Jetson and 4.0.1 on the Spark, so machine and version change together)" followed by "Whether the machine or the MuJoCo version moved it, no spec sheet would have predicted it". Either remove the comparison or measure one version on both machines.
6. A reference-manual structure (fault 6): an 11-row "Part / What it is / How you see it full" table, a 15 x 11 grid whose caption defines six cell words ("first", "also", "then", "or", "gate", "mix"), fifteen evidence paragraphs at equal weight, and a spread table ("the spread on each part runs from 16 times to more than 1,000"). Trivial facts sit beside decisive ones. The memory-latency paragraph measures 291.58 ns and 0.22 GB/s, then closes "None of the fifteen fills latency first.", so its numbers decide nothing.
7. The header promises "tell from its shape whether it can run on capacity AWS may take back", but the shapes table has no Spot verdict, and shape D ("Always on") is never told how to buy its capacity: "the scheduler and the way the capacity is bought do" stops before saying how. The payoff the header names is left for the reader to derive.
8. Provenance is a refrain: "(our single runs)", "(single runs)" three more times, and the header's "(our measurements are single runs)". The allowance is one plain clause, once.
9. "one environment steps 5.41 times a second on one RTX 5880 Ada, and 1,024 of them together 2,390 times: 2,390 / 5.41 = 442 times the rate of one from 1,024 copies, so copies packed on one card share it, where separate GPUs scale almost linearly." This is hard to parse ("share it": share what?), and its point is made again, cleanly, in the evaluation paragraph of the shapes section.
10. "Parts only the counters can name" re-teaches slots, front end and back end, the nvidia-smi definition and DCGM's 0.8 and 0.5 guidance, after the section has already sent the reader to "section 21, Reading a Machine" for exactly this. It is a compressed duplicate in the section that defers to the full version.

## doc-D: "4. Learning From Demonstrations: Why Copying Drifts and What Fixed It"

Scores
- argument 9: the first sentence is a claim ("fails for a reason its training loss never sees"), paragraph 2 works 600 decisions down to 6 and states 1% to 44%, and paragraph 3 turns both choices into a GPU bill. Every later block proves a piece of it.
- depth 9: the BC objective, the T-squared bound with toy numbers, DAgger, the chunk and play pair with real defaults, ensembling cost, the CVAE, diffusion and flow losses, FAST, a per-call time breakdown, and a failure-to-setting table.
- clarity 8: intuition, then formula, then decomposition, every time, with symbols defined before use and a meaning switch flagged. The gaps are one unexplained step (why a straight flow still takes 10 steps), symbol reuse handled by apology, and one symbol defined after its formula.
- sentences 8: often memorable ("Behaviour cloning is regression whose test set is written by its own mistakes."), but marred by authors-report and our-count tics.
- parity 9: the strongest file in the batch.

Findings, most damaging first
1. "The straightness is what saves steps: a step is a straight move, so it follows a straight route exactly however long it is, and leaves a curved one unless the steps are short." and "A−ε is the velocity along that line, the same at every τ, which is why the path is straight." If the route were straight, one Euler step would land exactly. The toy example does land exactly, and would in one step. The reader is never told why pi0 still takes 10: the learned field averages over every noise and demonstration pair passing through a point, so sampled paths bend where modes meet. This is a level-400 hole at the exact point the cost argument (1 against 10 against 100) rests on.
2. Provenance in the prose, the owner's own example family: "pi0 predicts 50 actions and, as its authors report, plays 25 of them"; "How each failure looks at the bench is our reading of its mechanism, here and in the closing table: this one is a smooth, confident reach..." (also self-referential); "6 x 50 = 300 tokens by our count" and "Against our count of 300 for this arm"; "By our arithmetic from LeRobot's tuning rule"; "The ACT authors report that they found the CVAE objective 'essential...'" (report that they found); and the header's "(the ACT, Diffusion Policy, pi0 and FAST papers, LeRobot v0.7.0 and the openpi code, read 2026-11-20)". The citations already carry this.
3. "In our reading, sampling one mode is a strong tendency on human demonstrations and falls short of a law." The claim is unclear. Is the tendency multimodal data, or the need for a sampling head? It closes the one paragraph that qualifies the section's second fix, so it needs a plain claim: a chunked regression head reached 97.1% on LIBERO, so a generative head is not always required.
4. "The authors report about 30 tokens per chunk per arm. Against our count of 300 for this arm that is 300 / 30 = 10 times fewer." FAST's 30 is for its own chunk length and joint count, while the 300 is this arm's 6 joints x 50 actions. Unless the chunks match, the 10x is a ratio of two different things. Give FAST's chunk, or compare its own naive count.
5. Symbol hygiene is handled by apology instead of fixed. "(in this paper k counts iterations, where ACT used it for the chunk length)" and "this ε is noise, a different quantity from the error rate in the compounding-error bound". The page already rewrites pi0 "in this page's symbols", so it can rename these too. In temporal ensembling, w_i = exp(-m i) appears before m is named. It is defined only by "The smaller m is, the flatter the weights" after the formula.
6. "The tensors are small." The panel's heading promises "the tensors", and the sentence asserts their size without giving one shape (image size, token count, the 100 x 6 output).
7. "A policy gives the machine two jobs of different shape, training and the call on the arm (section 20, Fifteen Workloads, Five Shapes), and both are matrix arithmetic on a GPU's tensor cores." This imports "shape", undefined here, and a training-cost detour (5 hours on a 2080 Ti, 4 days on 8 H100s) that delays the close. The ending paragraph after it is strong and would land harder without it.
8. "every token passes through the full 2B-parameter language model" and later "one run of a 3B-parameter token model" give two model sizes with nothing to reconcile them.
9. "Every policy described here is built on the first, and most of them on the second." "described here" is a small instance of prose that talks about itself, on the first screen.

## Batch answer

Yes. A careful reader would tell these files apart by quality alone. They fall into two groups.

Group 1: D, then A. Both open on a claim and work a number by paragraph 2. Both build each term before using it, give intuition before formula and say which term carries what, and end each number in a consequence or a setting ("halve T and it drops to a quarter"; "Compare shares only between cores of the same width; compare throughput between any two"). D is the more readable. A is denser and drifts into reference material in its EC2 and GPU-tool parts, but its rigour (recomputed card, known-answer test, width check) matches D's. Their faults are tics, not structure: D's "as its authors report" and "by our count", A's undefined "card" and one estimate placed before its formula.

Group 2: B and C, weaker for opposite reasons. B is clean and short with a real thesis, but it is a briefing for a reader who already knows prefill, decode, the KV cache, GEMMs and dense against sparse. It names techniques (GQA, SMEM/TMEM/SBUF, CUTLASS) where A and D explain mechanisms, and it never writes the roofline equation. C has as much evidence as A, laid out as a catalogue: a counting title, two theses, a taxonomy flagged as "a grouping of ours", a CPU-first thesis its own count undercuts, a sentence ("close to none of the three") that contradicts its arithmetic, and a cross-width share comparison that A's rule forbids.

Verdicts
- doc-D: sets the bar. Argument from the first sentence, intuition then formula then decomposition throughout, and an ending the reader acts on.
- doc-A: not distinguishable by quality from the best files. The same rigour and consequence-driven sentences, at a higher density and with a reference-manual stretch in the middle.
- doc-B: distinguishable, weaker. Tight sentences and a provable thesis, but the ramp is too steep for the stated reader, the levers are lists of names, and depth stops short of level 400.
- doc-C: distinguishable, weaker. Strong opening, then sprawl: a coined taxonomy, a thesis its own numbers undercut, a sentence that misstates its own arithmetic, and repeated provenance bookkeeping.
