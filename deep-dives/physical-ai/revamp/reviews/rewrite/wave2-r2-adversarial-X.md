## doc-A: "9. Collecting Data: What an Episode Weighs and What It Costs to Record"

Scores
- Argument 8: the claim ("the video is nearly all of it", and "the ratio is paid for in computing, twice") lands in paragraphs 1 and 2 with a number worked by paragraph 2. But the "paid twice in computing" half is never priced.
- Depth 8: reaches the mechanism (seek time = timestamp + from_timestamp, decode from the last keyframe), failure modes (frame drops, cadence drift, auto codec, skipped finalize) and config choices. Two of the trades it sets up are left with one side unquantified.
- Clarity 8: terms are built before use (keyframe, GOP, CRF, Parquet, hardware encoder). It leans on earlier sections for SO-101, teleoperation and policy, but glosses each one.
- Sentences 7: mostly specific and consequential. Some long nested sentences, a double "125" that confuses, and provenance clauses in the prose.
- Parity 8: publishable beside doc-B. Slightly more ledger and slightly less proof.

Findings (most damaging first)
1. "The ratio is paid for in computing, twice: once to compress, on the CPU cores of the host beside the arm, and again to decompress, every time a training job reads a frame." This is the section's thesis, and neither payment ever gets a compute number of its own. Encode load ends at vendor ratings: "In the sources read on 2026-11-20 the sizing stops at those two ratings, with no CPU-utilisation figure for the 640x480 row, so the first episode on your own host is the measurement." Decode gets one 5 ms figure, but that figure is never tied to a GOP, so it does not price the trade. The thesis is asserted and the disk and person costs are proved, while the compute cost the opening sets up stays qualitative. The fix is at least one encode figure (cores or frames per second for libsvtav1 at 640x480) or an explicit statement that no figure exists, given once.
2. "More keyframes buy that cheaper read with a larger file. The example dataset does not record its GOP, so to put a number on the size side for your own scene, record one episode at g = 2 and one at a long GOP and compare the two files." The keyframe section promises "one trade between the bench and the reader", but only the read side is numbered (125 times). The size side is handed to the reader as homework. A level 400 reader expects an order of magnitude for the file growth at g = 2.
3. "With a GOP of 250, a common setting for streamed video and used here as an illustration, the worst case is 250 decoded frames and the average about 250 / 2 = 125. Set against the at most 2 of a GOP of 2, the worst case is 250 / 2 = 125 times the decoding per random read." Two different quantities (the average decode count and the worst-case ratio) both come out as 125 from the same division, one sentence apart. The reader cannot tell which 125 matters. Give one of them, or name each one.
4. "Whoever types the record command at the bench, where a person drives the arm through each episode by teleoperation (section 3), picks the codec, the method that compresses the video and turns it back into frames, and how often the video stores a whole frame, and every later reader of the data inherits both." The appositive makes the list ambiguous: is "the method that compresses..." a third item? With "and ... and" and a cross-reference on top, a key sentence is hard to parse. Split it.
5. "The current docs still rate libsvtav1 "Smallest". A CRF of 30 is a different quality in each codec, so both sources can hold, and both point to the read ... its directory is gone from the repository at v0.7.0, so they rank the codecs and do not size a disk." This is source adjudication in the prose (fault 5). The reconciliation is also speculative, and it leaves the reader unsure whether the default codec makes smaller or larger files, which is the size side of the trade the section is about.
6. "an open LeRobot pull request timed one random sample from a two-camera, 50-episode dataset at about 5 ms, a median from one process with a warm file cache, on hardware it does not state." This is provenance bookkeeping inside the sentence that carries the number (fault 5). Most of these caveats belong in the citation. The one that matters for the reader (unknown hardware) can be said in four words.
7. "Its name says SO-101 while its metadata records the robot type as so100_follower, a value LeRobot uses for both the SO-100 and the SO-101 arm, so the files do not prove which of the two recorded it. Seven of the repository's nine files hold the data." These are trivia at the same weight as the numbers that decide a configuration (fault 6). Neither sentence changes what the reader does.
8. The sub-block "Size targets per file, and loading a v2.1 dataset" ("at most 100 MB per data Parquet file, 200 MB per video file, and 1,000 files per chunk-NNN directory") reads as a reference manual. The v2.1 refusal message is practical. The per-file limits mostly are not.
9. "A policy, the network that drives the arm, trained on that data and run at the nominal 30 Hz moves 1.2 times faster than the person did, by our reading." This restates the previous sentence ("the motion it holds is 30 / 25 = 1.2 times faster") and adds "by our reading". Either say what the policy does differently from the data, or cut it.
10. "The writer never splits an episode, so a few long ones make one to three files." The range is asserted, not shown. The reader cannot rebuild "one to three" from what is on the page.
11. "Version 2 of the format wrote one file per episode, so at the millions of episodes LeRobot's docs design for it meant millions of files". The consequence of millions of files (hub limits, file-system metadata, open calls per read) is left out. Without it this is a fact, not a reason.
12. "(In section 3 an encoder was the angle sensor on a joint; from here to the end of section 11 the word means the video compressor.)" The switch note is right to exist. "From here to the end of section 11" is prose about the document. "Here 'encoder' means the video compressor, not the joint's angle sensor" does the job.
13. Caption: "The same simulated episode from an overhead camera and a camera on the gripper." The dataset the section dissects has "side" and "up" cameras. The figure shows a different camera setup from the one in the table and the diagram, and its captions spend their words on capture provenance.
14. UMI's "1,400 / 12 = 117 per person-hour" is never set against the bench figure the section already has. At the example's own pace, 50 episodes in 56.6 person-minutes is about 53 per person-hour. That comparison is the number that would make "the larger saving" land.

## doc-B: "4. Learning From Demonstrations: Why Copying Drifts and What Fixed It"

Scores
- Argument 9: the first sentence makes the claim, paragraph 2 works it (600 to 6 decisions, 1% to 44%), and every block after it proves a piece of it. It ends on three numbers the reader writes down.
- Depth 9: three formulas, each with intuition first and a term-by-term decomposition, a toy worked example for each, the paper-versus-code divergences, the per-call time split (46 of 73 ms before the first flow step) and a failure table keyed to config values.
- Clarity 8: symbols are defined before formulas, and the k and epsilon collisions are flagged. Two crux points still jump: why z = 0 does not average, and why low-information tokens make training hard.
- Sentences 8: many carry consequences ("Behaviour cloning is regression whose test set is written by its own mistakes"). Weakened by about a dozen "the authors report" clauses in the prose, several "our count/our reading" clauses, and one muddled sentence on OpenVLA-OFT.
- Parity 9: the best file in the batch.

Findings (most damaging first)
1. "On the robot that second network is thrown away and z is set to zero, which gives one consistent way." The whole block exists to show that a regression head averages two modes into the obstacle, and ACT is offered as the one-pass fix. But at run time ACT is deterministic, and the reader is not told why a fixed z = 0 picks one mode instead of reproducing the average. The mechanism behind the claim is missing at the exact point where the reader needs it. Also, "a head that draws a sample is a generative head", yet ACT draws nothing at run time.
2. "The authors of OpenVLA-OFT took a 7B model that wrote actions as tokens, changed its head to predict the whole chunk in one parallel pass with a plain L1 regression loss ... In our reading, sampling one mode is a strong tendency on human demonstrations and falls short of a law." The evidence changes two things at once (chunking and the head), so it cannot speak to "how far the generative head is needed". The concluding sentence is the vaguest in the file ("a strong tendency ... falls short of a law") and carries "In our reading". State the confound, and say plainly what the result does and does not show.
3. "Neighbouring tokens barely differ when the motion is smooth, each one carries almost no new information, and the authors of FAST (...) report that it makes training hard at high control rates." This is the mechanism behind FAST, and it is skipped: why near-duplicate tokens make next-token training hard (the loss is dominated by copying the previous token) is never said. The reader gets a reported outcome where a level 400 page should give the reason.
4. The in-prose provenance is heavy (fault 5). There are about a dozen variants of "its authors report", "as its authors report", "The ACT authors report that they found", "The authors report", even though every citation marker already says "author reported". Others: "by our count" and "Against our count of 300", "How each failure looks at the bench is our reading of its mechanism, here and in the closing table", and "By our arithmetic from LeRobot's tuning rule". Keep the attribution where it grades a claim once. Elsewhere let the marker carry it.
5. "ACT in LeRobot v0.7.0: the tensors, the defaults you will type, and where the code overrides the paper" followed by "The tensors are small." No tensor shape follows, and chunk_size and n_action_steps repeat the table above. This is a reference-manual block (fault 6), saved by the n_decoder_layers = 1 versus 7 point.
6. "(in this paper k counts iterations, where ACT used it for the chunk length)" and "this ϵ is noise, a different quantity from the error rate in the compounding-error bound". The page flags its symbol collisions but keeps them. The level 200 reader has to hold two meanings each for k and epsilon across one section. Renaming (for example n for denoising iterations, ε_err for the error rate) removes the load.
7. "every token passes through the full 2B-parameter language model where a flow step runs a 300M-parameter action network" and, later, "one run of a 3B-parameter token model". The reader sees 2B and 3B for the same model line with no reconciliation.
8. "A policy gives the machine two jobs of different shape, training and the call on the arm (section 20, Fifteen Workloads, Five Shapes), and both are matrix arithmetic on a GPU's tensor cores." The penultimate paragraph is a tour of four cross-references (sections 20, 10, 15, 17), and "tensor cores" appears with no explanation.
9. "Those two choices are also the bill. ... and the time one call takes is distance the gripper travels on an old observation". This sentence opens the section's cost thesis, and it is clipped (it is missing an article and reads like shorthand).
10. "What a demonstration is as data, and how few of them a policy trained from scratch needs". This sub-heading description names a topic and gives no payoff. The paragraph under it ("Sit at the bench ... reach over the block, close the gripper, carry the block to the cup, let go") slips into a primer's register (fault 7), and "LeRobot's recorder stops at 50 episodes by default" is a trivial default at full weight.
11. "turning it on (temporal_ensemble_coeff, 0.01 in the paper) requires n_action_steps = 1." The text never says that temporal_ensemble_coeff is the m of the weight formula a few lines up, so the reader cannot connect the setting to the curve.
12. "The paper's algorithm box labels the first term MSE (mean squared error); its text and LeRobot's code use L1." This is paper-internal bookkeeping with no consequence for the reader, who has just been told the code uses L1.

## doc-C: "11. Augmentation and Synthetic Data: What Each Method Adds and the Machine It Needs"

Scores
- Argument 8: the governing claim ("Each method runs out of a different part of the machine first ... that part rules cards out before speed counts") arrives in paragraph 2 with a number (180.81 s against 59.83 s). The opening paragraph before it is a definitional tour.
- Depth 8: level 400 on yield economics, rendering on RT cores, memory floors and quadratic attention ("2.3 x 2.3 = 5.3 times the comparisons, and the measured 3.5 falls between the two"). Falls short on what drives yield, a confounded 30-fold comparison, and a G7e-versus-H100 decision left without any price.
- Clarity 7: "rendering" is used several times before it is defined. "latent-action model" is never explained. The header description front-loads RT cores, G7e and H100.
- Sentences 6: there are excellent sentences ("a generator writes that clip to disk like any other"), but many read as a ledger of vendor figures, with source adjudication in the prose, one plain slip ("one card has both") and figure text about the page itself.
- Parity 7: same structure and ambition as doc-A and doc-B, but it reads as a ledger more than an argument in its replay and memory blocks.

Findings (most damaging first)
1. "Suppose the SO-101 desktop arm has been driven through the task (pick up a block and place it in a cup) a few dozen times. Every episode (one recorded attempt at the task) shows the same table, the same lamp and a handful of block positions, so a policy (the network that turns observations into actions) learns the table and the lamp along with the task. Image transforms vary the picture of an episode you have; simulated replay makes new block positions; video generation gives an episode a new look." The first screen is three parenthetical re-definitions (in section 11) and a three-item tour of the page (faults 1, 2, 7). The first sentence is a good claim. The rest delays the thesis to paragraph 2.
2. Source adjudication runs through the prose (fault 5): "NVIDIA's figures do not say whether the 1,000 in its command, --generation_num_trials 1000, counts kept demonstrations or tries, so read the hour both ways. Kept, the reading the figure uses: ..." Also "AWS's list of June 2026, written against Isaac Sim 5.1, marks ...", "(a different card from the Blackwell one)", "though NVIDIA's methodology warns that equal model, GPU and resolution labels do not establish an identical workload", and the expandable "Cosmos 3 memory figures and what each one measures, for reference" (itself a reference-manual heading, fault 6), which is almost all provenance: "labels the models 8B and 32B where the model cards say 16B and 64B", "Both come from notes in NVIDIA's cosmos-framework repository", "NVIDIA calls these measured setups, not memory requirements", "at a speed cost the README does not quantify". Some of it is load-bearing (the 2.5 times uncertainty on the replay hour). Most of it is the writer's research notes left in.
3. "To put money on either side, multiply its hours by a dated hourly price for your Region and purchase option, charging the whole instance even when a job uses part of it." The title promises "the Machine It Needs", and the closing decision (share one G7e, or buy a separate H100) is left with no price at all. The rule "while the extra generation hours fit in the time the card would otherwise sit idle" is qualitative. One dated price pair would turn the decision into arithmetic.
4. "Per demonstration that is 1.1 GPU-seconds against 36, a 30-fold spread between two tasks, so a figure from another task cannot size yours." It compares a humanoid task whose camera setting is not stated with a nut-pouring task "with cameras", two paragraphs after the page showed that cameras alone cost 4 times. Part of the 30-fold may be cameras, so the conclusion is drawn from a confounded pair.
5. "Isaac Lab's docs put the yield between about 70% in simple cases and under 1%: one divided by the yield gives 2.5 candidates per kept demonstration at the 40% NVIDIA reports for cube stacking, and 100 at 1%." The colon joins a range to a calculation at a value outside the range's endpoints. Worse, the heading says "the yield sets the bill", yet nothing tells the reader what makes yield fall from 70% to under 1% (horizon length, precision of the success rule, the number of segments joined), so the reader cannot predict their own yield.
6. "Transforms need no new card, replay needs RT cores and generation needs memory, and one card has both." There are three items and then "both". It is a careless slip in the sentence that opens the decision section.
7. "Drawing what each simulated camera sees is called rendering". The definition comes after "a GPU that renders" (sub-heading), "the ray-tracing units it renders with" (paragraph 2) and "GPU rendering" (figure). The term is used before it is built.
8. Figure text: "No generated clip is shown: none was produced for this page.", "our estimate: milliseconds per image. No published figure", and in the caption "Row 2's randomised MuJoCo scenes, from a different capture, stand in for simulated replays ... the transform time is our estimate." The figure talks about itself and its own sourcing (faults 4, 5), and it puts an unsourced estimate on a log axis beside measured figures. The section's sharpest claim, "Only replay checks what it makes", is used as this figure's heading. Its description ("how far apart their times per new sample sit on a log axis") describes the chart, and the claim is not argued until much later ("Neither way has the simulator's test").
9. "Check that Mimic is registered for that task before planning around it." This hands the reader a check the page could have answered from the repository it cites. It reads as unfinished research.
10. "Hugging Face's v0.7.0 release post puts the gain of the move at 2.8 times for ACT ... on an H100 and 3.3 times on an A100". It never says gain in what (training steps per second? wall-clock time?). The sentence spends its words on what the post omits and leaves out the metric.
11. "NVIDIA gives about 9.5 GB of GPU memory for one environment and about 22 GB for five, recommends at least 24 GB for one or two environments and 48 GB for up to about five, and states that the gain from more than one environment is not very significant, so a 24 GB card running one environment is the buy its figures point to." This is a 60-word chain of vendor figures. The conclusion is good and buried at the tail.
12. "In the same table's rows for Diffusers, ... the RTX PRO 6000 repaints a 480p clip in 217.90 s against the H100's 132.06 s". The ratio (about 1.65 times, against 3.3 times on the PyTorch path) is the number that changes the decision, and it is left uncomputed.
13. "Cosmos3-Nano, its 16-billion-parameter middle size". "Middle" implies a smaller model that is never named. "RTX Pro 6000" and "RTX PRO 6000" both appear. "an inverse-dynamics model (...) or a latent-action model" explains the first term and drops the second.
14. Header description: "Pick the method by the failure the policy shows and the GPU by RT cores and memory: one G7e runs both simulated replay and video generation, at three to four times an H100's generation time (LeRobot v0.7.0, Isaac Lab v3.0.0 and NVIDIA's Cosmos 3 pages, read 2026-11-20)". The payoff is strong, but it is crowded with unbuilt terms and a source parenthetical.

## doc-D: "Memory Hierarchy Primer"

Scores
- Argument 6: subsections carry claims ("The bandwidth wall sits at HBM", "Bandwidth per core can fall as cores rise"), and paragraph 2 works a sharp number (75 times less bandwidth for under twice the capacity). The page as a whole is a tour of tiers, opened by a definition under run-in labels.
- Depth 6: many figures, little mechanism. The ridge point is asserted, not worked. HBF's sweep rate is computed and then not used. Failure modes are almost absent apart from per-core bandwidth.
- Clarity 4: the level 100 to 200 reader is lost early. "operands", "the unit executes", "working set", SM, "tiles", "tcgen05", "ridge point", "decode", "lines", "replacement policy", "occupancy", "bounce buffer", "Pond", "16-high", "write endurance" and "KV prefixes" go unexplained. SRAM, DRAM, NVMe, HBM, KV, GDS and NUMA are expanded and never explained.
- Sentences 6: compact and number-bearing (the Graviton paragraph is excellent), but there are fragments, pointer-endings and run-in labels.
- Parity 5: clearly below the other three for this reader.

Findings (most damaging first)
1. The header promises "what each step down from registers to NVMe flash costs in bandwidth and latency", and the map does not deliver it. The bandwidth column is "-" for all five on-chip tiers (register file, SRAM scratchpad, L1, L2, LLC). Latencies mix H800 cycles ("~479 cycles") with nanoseconds on other platforms ("~144 ns"), so no two adjacent steps can be compared without a clock rate the page never gives. The chart's own sub-heading, "Read capacity, bandwidth and latency per tier off one chart", promises a reading the chart cannot support.
2. "A first-generation stack can be read end to end about 3 times a second, a shipping HBM4 stack about 80 times ... That ratio decides what HBF can hold (High Bandwidth Flash)." This is the page's best tool, and the consequence is deferred to another section (and the pointer reads like an acronym expansion). Then the close says "bytes read every decode step go in HBM, or HBF once it ships", and the header description says "High Bandwidth Flash for bytes read every step". [My derivation: if weights that fill a stack are read every decode step, three sweeps a second caps decode near three tokens a second per full stack.] The rule and the ratio pull against each other, and the page never reconciles them.
3. "What the hierarchy is. Every architecture in this deep dive answers one question: where do the operands live when the unit executes?" and then "How it behaves." The title is a topic label with a primer's name. The opening is a definition under a run-in label (fault 1), the first sentence talks about the deep dive (fault 4), and "operands" and "the unit" are undefined for the stated reader.
4. Expansion is used as if it were explanation, throughout: "on-chip SRAM (static RAM)", "NVMe (Non-Volatile Memory Express)", "HBM (High Bandwidth Memory)", "KV (key-value) cache", "NUMA (non-uniform memory access) locality". A reader who does not know what a KV cache is learns nothing from "key-value", yet the page's closing placement rule depends on it ("KV prefixes read once per cache hit").
5. "An inference accelerator pairs 3.35 TB/s (H100) to 22 TB/s (Rubin) of HBM per GPU with enough tensor throughput that decode, which reads every weight for each token, sits below the ridge point (Roofline and Arithmetic Intensity)." The central claim of "The bandwidth wall sits at HBM" rests on an unexplained term, and no number is worked (no FLOP per byte for the chip or for decode). "Raising HBM bandwidth is expensive" is never quantified.
6. Pointers replace consequences about ten times in 1,800 words (fault 4): "NVIDIA Blackwell: B200 and B300 and AWS Trainium, Inferentia and Neuron cover how kernels use each.", "Grace-Blackwell and UltraServer covers offloading KV ...", "(DDR5, MRDIMM, LPDDR5X and CXL)", "Graviton Deep Dive has the Graviton numbers", "Flash as a Cache Tier for Inference shows when reading KV beats recomputing it." The page ends on one: "The vLLM dive's KV Offloading and LMCache section covers the software that moves KV between these tiers." It does not end on the reader's action.
7. "Trainium2's SBUF holds 28 MiB per NeuronCore, about 126 times a Hopper SM's 228 KB of SMEM". This compares a per-NeuronCore figure with a per-SM figure. A Hopper GPU has over a hundred SMs, so chip for chip the gap is far smaller. As written, the 126 times misleads.
8. "GB200's NVLink-C2C raises that link to 900 GB/s bidirectional" set against "64 GB/s per direction". Mixing bidirectional and per-direction figures leaves the reader to halve one of them before comparing. The step is never stated.
9. "Blackwell's TMEM holds the tiles its tcgen05 tensor-core instructions work on." This is a jargon sentence with no meaning for the stated reader (tiles, tcgen05, tensor-core instructions).
10. "It is the tier most often missed, and the one accelerators build on." It is vague (missed by whom?) and unsupported.
11. "in Pond's estimates 70 to 90 ns extra in pools of 8 to 16 sockets, over 180 ns at rack scale." "Pond" is never introduced.
12. Caption: "Rubin's 22 TB/s is the per-GPU figure on NVIDIA's HGX page; the Vera Rubin NVL72 page gives 19.2 TB/s. ... the per-family table below and Capacity beyond HBM and DRAM cite the rest. A hyphen marks no sourced figure; HBF is dashed because no product ships." The caption is mostly source bookkeeping and directions about the page (faults 4, 5).
13. The per-family table puts facts at equal weight, with many "-" cells and "Per core" in place of a CPU register-file figure (fault 6). It repeats the map more than it adds a consequence.
14. "at no less than 2.25 GB/s per GPU; FSx for Lustre with GDS reaches 18.75 GB/s per GPU, EBS 1.56." The sentence trails into a fragment ("EBS 1.56"), and the instance-store figure is missing from the map row it should match (1.56 to 18.75).
15. "Spilling past HBM costs 75 times the bandwidth for under twice the capacity". This is loose: it means 75 times less bandwidth. Paragraph 2 says it correctly. "Our reading of these figures:" is bookkeeping in the closing rule.

## Batch answer

Groups by quality alone:
- Group 1, doc-B and doc-A. Each opens on a claim and works a number by the second paragraph. Each builds a mechanism before configuration (B: the bound, chunking, three loss formulas with decompositions; A: decode from the last keyframe, seek time as an addition), and each ends on what the reader types or checks. B is the stronger of the two because it proves its claims with formulas and toy numbers the reader can redo. A leaves two of its trades with one side unpriced.
- Group 1, lower edge, doc-C. It has the same skeleton and the same level 400 reach in places (yield, RT cores, memory floors, quadratic attention). It reads more like a ledger of vendor figures: source adjudication left in the prose, a definitional tour as the first paragraph, figure text about the page itself, a careless "one card has both", and the closing hardware decision handed to the reader without a price.
- Group 2, doc-D. It is a compressed reference primer: a map whose bandwidth column is empty on-chip and whose latencies mix cycles with nanoseconds, acronyms expanded and never explained, about ten cross-references standing in for consequences, a placement rule that its own HBF sweep-rate figure contradicts, and an ending that is a pointer.

Would a careful reader tell them apart by quality alone? Yes. doc-D is plainly weaker for the stated reader: they would be lost by the second paragraph, and the page delivers figures without the mechanism that makes them usable. doc-C is distinguishable only narrowly: the reasoning is as deep as doc-A's, but its sentences carry more bookkeeping and less consequence. doc-A and doc-B read as one standard.

Verdicts:
- doc-A: not distinguishable by quality from the best files (claim-led, numbers worked early, mechanism before configuration; its gaps are two trades with one side unpriced).
- doc-B: sets the bar (argument from the first sentence, every formula built from intuition and decomposed, failure table keyed to settings).
- doc-C: distinguishable: weaker, narrowly (same depth, but ledger sentences, provenance in the prose, a tour opening and an unpriced decision).
- doc-D: distinguishable: weaker (level 100 to 200 reader lost; map does not deliver its promised comparison; pointers in place of consequences).
