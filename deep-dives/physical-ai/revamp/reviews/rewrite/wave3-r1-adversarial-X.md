## doc-A: "16. Evaluation: Counting Successes, and How Many Trials It Takes"

Scores
- argument 9: the claim (a few dozen trials cannot separate two good policies) is made in paragraph 2 with the number that proves it (p = 0.26, 199 trials per policy), and every later block cashes it out.
- depth 9: Wilson centre and half-width decomposed, Fisher's test, power and sample size, saturation, perturbation sweeps, seeds and room drift, Batch array jobs on Spot, RT cores and instance family. All the arithmetic reproduces (Wilson 67.0-88.8 and 78.6-95.7, Fisher p = 0.262 and 0.0228, n = 198.7 and 390.8, Wald 0.714-1.086, the 12-of-12 lower end 0.76, the 500-episode intervals, 86 GPU-seconds).
- clarity 8: confidence interval, p-value, power, Pearson and Spearman are each built before use. A few terms come in from other sections with no working gloss, and RoboArena appears before it is introduced.
- sentences 8: mostly specific and consequential ("Last month's count is no baseline."). There is some arithmetic-as-prose and some repeated provenance clauses.
- parity 9: this is the bar.

Findings, most damaging first
1. The ladder figure claims an order the text does not support. Its caption has to retract its own layout: "the vertical order is the ladder's, and the correlations alone do not put one rung above the other." The axis labels still say "trusted most, slowest to run" at the top and put world models (rung two) above simulation (rung one), and nothing in the prose argues that a world model is more trusted or slower than a simulator. A figure that needs its caption to disown it is mis-designed. Fix: order the rungs by cost only and say so, or drop the rung numbering.
2. A term is used before it is built, and a count is left unreconciled. "RoboWorld runs this from the first frame of each RoboArena episode": RoboArena is only introduced in the next paragraph. That paragraph then quotes "more than 600 pairwise real-robot evaluation episodes across seven generalist policies" and follows with "RoboWorld's replication of the leaderboard covers 8 policies." A careful reader stops at 7 against 8 and gets no explanation.
3. Provenance bookkeeping repeats in the prose (owner's fault 5). Examples: "the authors of ABC, a 2026 study of behaviour cloning, report a validation loss that went down and then up"; "Each figure comes from the authors of the tool it describes."; the figure labels "Pearson r with success on real robots, as each paper reports it" and "0.989 RoboWorld, each as its own authors report"; "(an illustration)" twice; "MuJoCo stills made for this dive: scripted motion, no learned policy." The self-reported-figure caveat carries weight once. Here it is said four times.
4. The section leans on terms from section 4 with no working gloss: "for a diffusion policy it is the error in predicting the added noise (section 4, Learning From Demonstrations)", "runs the policy as it runs on the arm, sampler included", and later "at a fixed number of denoising steps". A reader who starts here meets "sampler" and "denoising steps" as bare words, and the ABC caveat about holding steps fixed depends on them. One clause would fix it.
5. The expandable "Why the offline number flatters a model: an AWS customer engagement" is a chain of quotes with no context. "The offline setup "feeds the model ground-truth video frames"": which setup, and what is DreamZero? The panel adds no mechanism or figure that the open-loop argument above has not already given, and its heading is framed around the source rather than the claim.
6. "Choosing among 5 checkpoints of one run (an illustration), at 199 trials each, is at least 5 x 199 = 995 attempts at the task, since picking the best of five makes several comparisons rather than one". The multiple-comparisons argument is squeezed into a "since" clause. The reader is not told why several comparisons raise the count (each one is another chance of a false alarm, so each needs a stricter level), and the figure quietly assumes every pair is 10 points apart.
7. Arithmetic is written as prose: "stepped 64 to 320 simulated environments in parallel on eight H100 GPUs, 64 / 8 = 8 to 320 / 8 = 40 per GPU against the 8,192 of Isaac Lab's state-only benchmark". It reads as an equation chain. Write "8 to 40 per GPU".
8. Cross-reference density. About a dozen "(section N, Title)" pointers, one of which carries a whole clause about another section: "(section 24, Orchestrating Physical AI on AWS, runs one policy's evaluation that way)". This is the light form of fault 4.
9. Depth gap at a decision point. STEP's sequential test is recommended in words ("an easy comparison ends early, a hard one runs on") with no figure for how many trials it saves. That is the number a reader would need before choosing it over a fixed count.
10. The header description ends in bookkeeping: "(LeRobot v0.7.0, sources read 2026-11-20)".
11. "The open-loop error does not fully capture three closed-loop effects: compounding errors, contact dynamics and recovery ability." This is a paraphrased triad with a weak verb ("does not fully capture"). The next two sentences rescue it by tying contact to the dropped block.

## doc-B: "Memory Hierarchy Primer"

Scores
- argument 5: it opens on a definition and a map. The one tool it offers ("Place each byte by how often it is read") arrives in the last paragraph. Two subsections ("The bandwidth wall sits at HBM", "Bandwidth per core can fall as cores rise") do make claims, and only the second proves its claim.
- depth 6: there are many numbers, and some are well used: the Graviton per-core arithmetic reproduces (844.8/192 = 4.4 against 537.6/96 = 5.6 GB/s), as do HBF's ~3 full reads a second against HBM4's ~80. The mechanisms are asserted rather than shown. The "bandwidth wall" gets no FLOPs-per-byte figure, and "Raising HBM bandwidth is expensive" gets no number.
- clarity 4: a level 100-200 reader is lost at "decode", "ridge point", "KV cache", "tiles", "tcgen05", "occupancy", "Pond" and "MRDIMM". The map's latency column mixes units that cannot be compared.
- sentences 5: there are crisp lines ("Divide socket bandwidth by busy cores before moving a bandwidth-bound service to a bigger CPU"). There are also template labels, section titles glued into sentences, and one sentence that says the opposite of what it means.
- parity 4: a reference primer next to argued sections.

Findings, most damaging first
1. It opens on a definition and a tour, with template labels (owner's fault 1): "What the hierarchy is. Every architecture in this deep dive answers one question: where do the operands live when the unit executes? The memory hierarchy is the map for comparing the answers". The next paragraph opens "How it behaves." The labels read as scaffolding left in, "in this deep dive" is the page talking about itself, and "the unit" is never defined.
2. The map cannot be read as a map. Latency is in H800 cycles for SMEM, L1, L2 and HBM ("~29 cycles", "~263 cycles", "~479 cycles") and in nanoseconds for LLC, DRAM and CXL ("~33 ns", "~144 ns", "~254 ns"), and no clock is given to convert between them. Bandwidth is "-" for five of the ten tiers. The header promises "what each step down from registers to NVMe flash costs in bandwidth and latency", and the table cannot give the cost of the step from L2 to LLC or from HBM to DRAM. Some rows also splice CPU capacity with GPU latency: "L1 cache ... 48-64 KB / data, per CPU core ... ~41 cycles / GPU L1, H800".
3. The second table reads like a reference manual (owner's fault 6): facts at equal weight, and many cells that decide nothing. "Register file | Per core | 256 KB per SM | -", "CXL 2.0 pooling, CXL 3.0 sharing", "None documented above L2". No row says which fact changes a choice.
4. The central claim is asserted on terms the reader does not have: "decode, which reads every weight for each token, sits below the ridge point (Roofline and Arithmetic Intensity)". "Ridge point" is never explained, "decode" is never introduced, and no number shows the wall (no ratio of compute to bandwidth against decode's bytes per FLOP). Others in the same family: "KV (key-value) cache" (expanded, not explained), "Blackwell's TMEM holds the tiles its tcgen05 tensor-core instructions work on.", "Shows up as a schedule slip at compile time (Trainium) or a lower-occupancy kernel (GPU)", "in Pond's estimates 70 to 90 ns extra" (Pond is never introduced), "(DDR5, MRDIMM, LPDDR5X and CXL)".
5. Section titles are glued into sentences as bare text, which garbles them and defers the payoff elsewhere (owner's fault 4). "NVIDIA Blackwell: B200 and B300 and AWS Trainium, Inferentia and Neuron cover how kernels use each." "That ratio decides what HBF can hold (High Bandwidth Flash).": the sentence promises a consequence and hands it to another section, and the parenthesis reads like a second acronym expansion. The section ends on a pointer to another dive: "The vLLM dive's KV Offloading and LMCache section covers the software that moves KV between these tiers."
6. A misleading ratio: "Trainium2's SBUF holds 28 MiB per NeuronCore, about 126 times a Hopper SM's 228 KB of SMEM". It compares one NeuronCore with one of a Hopper GPU's 132 SMs [my count; the file does not give it]. The unit choice inflates the gap the sentence uses to argue that scratchpads matter.
7. 900 GB/s is used two ways. The second table has "NVLink-C2C, 900 GB/s bidirectional (GB200)" for host DRAM and "900 GB/s each way" for NVLink to other GPUs in the next row, and both are set against "64 GB/s per direction". The reader cannot tell which 900 has to be halved before comparing.
8. "Spilling past HBM costs 75 times the bandwidth for under twice the capacity" literally says spilling uses 75 times more bandwidth. The intended meaning is a 75-fold drop. The first paragraph had it right ("75 times less bandwidth").
9. Bookkeeping in prose and caption (owner's fault 5): "Our reading of these figures:"; the caption's "Rubin's 22 TB/s is the per-GPU figure on NVIDIA's HGX page; the Vera Rubin NVL72 page gives 19.2 TB/s." and "A hyphen marks no sourced figure; HBF is dashed because no product ships."
10. The thesis is buried (owner's fault 3), and the advice runs ahead of the hedge. "Place each byte by how often it is read." is the section's one decision rule, and it is in the final paragraph. The subsection description tells the reader to "Choose ... High Bandwidth Flash for bytes read every step" although the body says "No HBF product ships as of October 2026".
11. Unsupported generalities: "It is the tier most often missed, and the one accelerators build on." (missed by whom, on what evidence?). In the cache/scratchpad figure, "Scratchpad miss / Cannot happen at run time" names a thing and then says it cannot exist. "Cache miss ... Shows up as tail latency at run time" overgeneralises: a miss costs stalls and throughput, not specifically tail latency.

## doc-C: "15. Training at Three Scales: One GPU, One Node, Many Nodes"

Scores
- argument 9: the thesis is in the first paragraph and stated as a tool by the fourth ("What trains sets the memory floor ... The wall-clock you can wait divides the GPU-hours into a width, and the width decides the network, the checkpoints and the market"). The section ends on the action that applies it.
- depth 9: it derives about 16 bytes per trainable parameter from two openpi memory figures and checks the result against NVIDIA's 80 GB advice. It also covers samples per second against vendor rates, gradient-exchange time against network bandwidth, Spot rules, Capacity Blocks and training-plan eligibility, EFA and zone limits, and the LeRobot flag pitfall. The arithmetic reproduces (30,720/3.4 = 9,035; (70 - 22.5)/3 = 15.8; 3.14 x 18 = 57; 52 and 22 samples/s; 768/24 = 32 days; 6.3 GB at 50 Gbps = 1.0 s and at 3,200 Gbps = 16 ms; 1.28M/192.57 = 1.8 h).
- clarity 8: a training step, gradient, optimiser state, activations, LoRA, FSDP and EFA are each built at first use. One antecedent is ambiguous, and two chart rows are never introduced.
- sentences 8: mostly consequence-bearing. There is more provenance bookkeeping in parentheses than A or D carry.
- parity 8: same method and quality as the best files, with two comparisons that are mis-set.

Findings, most damaging first
1. The section's strongest failure story computes the small number and leaves the big one implicit. "It hit its 40-hour wall-clock cap at step 83 of 100 ... the instance terminated with the checkpoints of steps 25, 50 and 75 still on its own disk." Then: "The eight steps after the last save alone were about 8 x 27 = 216 minutes on eight GPUs, 29 GPU-hours, and a save on the machine's own disk dies with the machine." By the paragraph's own facts every checkpoint died with the instance, so the loss was the whole run: about 40 h x 8 = 320 GPU-hours [my arithmetic]. The word "alone" gestures at this and the number never lands.
2. A comparison mixes two different jobs. A 6.3 GB full-weight gradient exchange is set against the step time of a frozen-backbone run: "For scale, a step of AWS's frozen-backbone run took about 32 / 52 = 0.62 s: a second of exchange would more than double a step of that length, and 16 ms would add about 0.016 / 0.62 = 3%." A frozen-backbone step does not produce a 6.3 GB gradient, and a full fine-tune step does not take 0.62 s, so "more than double" and "3%" describe no real job. Fix: pair the 6.3 GB with a full fine-tune step time, or pair 0.62 s with the frozen run's actual gradient size.
3. Provenance bookkeeping and self-reference in the prose (owner's faults 4 and 5): the header's "(papers, model cards, LeRobot v0.7.0 and AWS posts, read 2026-11-20)"; "(a single run, not a speed benchmark)"; "(from the model card's energy estimate, which may sum several runs)"; "The fabric has its own dive (EFA), and so does the way a step is split across nodes and what each trained weight costs in bytes (Training at Scale)."; "Section 24, Orchestrating Physical AI on AWS, picks the scheduler for each."
4. An ambiguous antecedent, and an instruction with no method. "The setting is a command-line flag, and it has failed without warning." follows a paragraph about gradient checkpointing, but "the setting" means the full / LoRA / frozen choice from two paragraphs earlier. The pitfall is then closed with "Confirm which weights a run trains before trusting a memory figure for it." without saying how to confirm it (for example, the trainable-parameter count a run reports at start).
5. A padding formula. "GPU-hours = number of GPUs × hours of wall-clock time" is set as a display equation. It is level-0 arithmetic given the visual weight of the Wilson or flow-matching formulas elsewhere. The sentence after it, about which factor decides what, is the content.
6. Chart rows the prose never introduces: "GR00T 2 LoRA, one RTX 5090" and "X-VLA pretraining" exist only in the figure. A reader who has just learned GR00T N1 and N1.7 meets "GR00T 2" with no idea what it is.
7. "Compare runs in samples, not steps." The example gives the numbers but not the wrong conclusion it guards against. By steps the g6e run looks 18% slower (4.0 h against 3.4 h); per sample it is 2.4 times slower (22 against 52 a second) [my arithmetic]. That is the payoff sentence, and it is missing.
8. A quote stands in for a number: gradient checkpointing is recommended "because it "reduces memory usage significantly"", with no figure for the memory saved or the compute added. This is the lever the closing section tells the reader to try second.
9. A figure that cannot be redone as written: "at the recorder's default of 60 s per episode the same 50 episodes are 90,000 frames". The 30 frames a second that makes 90,000 is not stated in that paragraph.
10. Taxonomy framing at the top: "Jobs come in three sizes" in paragraph 1, and "Three numbers place any job. What trains sets the memory floor". "What trains" is not a number. The body earns the taxonomy, but the opening reads as a list before it reads as an argument.
11. A reference-manual paragraph (owner's fault 6): "For PyTorch on HyperPod clusters run by EKS, managed tiered checkpointing keeps checkpoints in the nodes' CPU memory, copies them to adjacent nodes and periodically persists them to durable storage such as S3." It describes a feature with no number and no decision, and sits at the same weight as the EFA-zone constraint that actually rules configurations out.

## doc-D: "4. Learning From Demonstrations: Why Copying Drifts and What Fixed It"

Scores
- argument 9: the first paragraph states the failure, its mechanism and its growth law, and the second paragraph lands a measured number (1% to 44%). Every subsection then either proves a link or turns it into a setting.
- depth 9: the BC objective and the T²ε bound are decomposed term by term, with toy numbers (0.6 against 360, and 3.6 with chunks); there is DAgger and why it is not used, temporal ensembling and its cost in calls, the CVAE loss, diffusion and flow losses, the FAST trade, the per-call time breakdown of pi0 (46 of 73 ms before the first flow step), and a failure-to-setting table. The arithmetic reproduces (6 x 6 x 0.001 = 0.036; 46/73 = 63%; 2 x 73 = 146 ms; 0.25 x 0.75 = 188 mm; 1/0.073 = 13.7).
- clarity 8: symbols are defined before each formula, intuition comes before formula, and the second sense of "multimodal" is flagged. The reader is let down at two steps (flow straightness and ACT's z = 0), and symbol reuse is handled by warnings.
- sentences 8: many lines carry the argument ("Behaviour cloning is regression whose test set is written by its own mistakes."). Provenance tics are frequent.
- parity 9: this is the bar.

Findings, most damaging first
1. The straightness argument contradicts the step count it is meant to explain. "a step is a straight move, so it follows a straight route exactly however long it is", and later "the same at every τ, which is why the path is straight". If the sampled route were straight, one Euler step would finish the chunk, yet the page says pi0 takes 10 and never says why. The straight line holds for each training pair of noise and demonstration. The field the network learns averages over many pairs and bends, which is why sampling still needs several steps. The toy example (0.8 to 0.2 in ten equal steps) reinforces the wrong picture. Fix: one sentence saying the learned route is close to straight, not straight, and that this is why it takes ten steps and not a hundred, but not one.
2. ACT's commitment to one mode is asserted, not shown. "On the robot that second network is thrown away and z is set to zero, which gives one consistent way." The later decomposition shows only that zero is a value the decoder met in training. It does not show why the decoder at z = 0 picks one route rather than the average of the two, which is the failure the subsection opened on. The opening frame also counts ACT among the samplers ("The second change is to sample one of several valid motions ... ACT needs 1."), although at run time ACT does not sample.
3. Bookkeeping in the prose, close to the owner's own examples (fault 5): "How each failure looks at the bench is our reading of its mechanism, here and in the closing table"; "6 x 50 = 300 tokens by our count"; "Against our count of 300 for this arm"; "In our reading, sampling one mode is a strong tendency..."; "By our arithmetic from LeRobot's tuning rule"; "pi0 predicts 50 actions and, as its authors report, plays 25 of them on 50 Hz robots"; "The ablation passage gives no reason for the taper"; "the paper notes, citing earlier work, that problems exist where it is reached".
4. The closing caveat is garbled where it matters most: "In our reading, sampling one mode is a strong tendency on human demonstrations and falls short of a law." The subject is wrong: what is in question is whether a sampling head is needed, and the sentence says sampling is a tendency. It is also the only place the OpenVLA-OFT result (76.5% to 97.1% with plain L1 regression) meets the opening claim that "most of them" are built on the second fix, and the tension is left as a vague hedge.
5. A ratio that may not compare like with like: "The authors report about 30 tokens per chunk per arm. Against our count of 300 for this arm that is 300 / 30 = 10 times fewer." FAST's chunk length and joint count are not given, so the tenfold figure rests on an unstated assumption that both chunks are the same.
6. "Tick" means two things: the page uses tick for a control step throughout, and the flow figure says "one tick is one pass of the network".
7. The deadline is stated two ways without reconciling them. "It has to return before the chunk in play runs out, every time" against the ending's "has to stay under about half the duration of the chunk it predicts". The reader is not told that the first is the synchronous case and the second the asynchronous one, and the derivation is deferred to section 17.
8. Symbol collisions are handled by warnings instead of renaming: "(in this paper k counts iterations, where ACT used it for the chunk length)" and "this ε is noise, a different quantity from the error rate in the compounding-error bound". The warnings are correct, but two of them in one section cost the level-200 reader. Since the page already rewrites pi0's formulas "in this page's symbols", it could rename here too.
9. The penultimate paragraph is routing: "(section 20, Fifteen Workloads, Five Shapes) ... (section 10, Processing and Curating) ... section 15, Training at Three Scales, sorts the sizes". It restates training costs already given (5 hours on a 2080 Ti) in order to point elsewhere, just before the ending.
10. A subsection description that restates the topic instead of the payoff: "What a demonstration is as data, and how few of them a policy trained from scratch needs".
11. Quotes stand in for evidence: the CVAE is "essential in learning precise tasks from human demonstrations", where the ablation behind "essential" has figures that could be given. "a well-known challenge for policy learning" is a citation on an undisputed point that adds weight and no information.
12. The header description ends in a provenance parenthesis: "(the ACT, Diffusion Policy, pi0 and FAST papers, LeRobot v0.7.0 and the openpi code, read 2026-11-20)".

## Batch answer: groups by quality alone

**Group 1, the bar: doc-A, doc-D, and doc-C with them.** Each opens on an argument and works a number by the second paragraph (A: p = 0.26 and 199 trials; D: T²ε and 1% to 44%; C: 3.4 GPU-hours against 30,720, and the floor/width rule by paragraph four). Each builds formulas intuition first, with arithmetic the reader can redo. Each turns mechanism into a setting or a purchase (pc_success_ci95 and Spot array jobs; num_inference_steps and use_vae; --peft.method_type and Capacity Blocks), and each ends on the reader's next action. C sits a notch below A and D on finish: two mis-set comparisons (the 29 GPU-hours that should be about 320; the frozen-backbone step against a full-weight gradient) and more provenance parentheses. A careful reader could not pick it out of this group by quality.

**Group 2: doc-E.** The first three paragraphs, the half-chunk rule and the RTC mechanism match group 1. What separates it is finish, not method: a dated placeholder block that says it is unfinished and was never run on a device; a running example (GR00T N1.7 on an Orin, driven by LeRobot's async settings) that the page itself later shows sits on a different stack from LeRobot's; a Greengrass graft that argues a different thesis under this title; a misattribution ("The paper rounds the 149.0 ms Orin median"); and a "measure it yourself" refrain. A careful reader tells it apart at the placeholder, and from the late caveat about the running example.

**Group 3: doc-B.** It is weaker on every axis that separates group 1 from the rest. It opens on template labels and a definition ("What the hierarchy is." / "How it behaves."). The tables give every fact equal weight. The latency column mixes cycles and nanoseconds with no way to compare them.
