## 1. What HBF is, mechanically

**Summary for the author.** HBF is a stack of NAND dies (the "Core die") on a logic "Base die", joined by TSVs, that sits on the accelerator package and talks to the xPU over UCIe die-to-die links rather than over the JEDEC HBM interface. The Base die is a flash controller: it runs the UCIe PHY and protocol, does ECC, schedules NAND operations, manages bad blocks and TSV repair, and counts reads per block for refresh. Wear levelling is either Base-die-driven (vendor-specific) or host-controlled by "zone remapping"; there is **no garbage collection**, and the host must write each NAND block sequentially. The OCP specification is a high-level base-die specification at version 0.7.0, so many device parameters (endurance, read-disturb limit, block size, latency, power) are deferred to "product specific" datasheets that no one has published.

## 2. Who is building and standardising it

**Summary for the author.** HBF is a Sandisk invention (announced 2025-02-11) that became a two-vendor standard with SK hynix, run under the Open Compute Project (OCP) rather than JEDEC. The OCP specification lists Sandisk and SK hynix as contributors and acknowledges Google and Tenstorrent; Sandisk's CTO announced Meta as a consortium member on 2026-08-13. Samsung and Kioxia have each said they are working on stacked-NAND memory of their own, outside the OCP specification as far as this search found. No GPU or accelerator vendor (NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, AWS, Google TPU product line) has announced a product that uses HBF; Google's participation is in the standard and on a conference panel, not a product. Sandisk keeps HBF out of its revenue model and its FY2026 10-K does not mention it.

## 3. Numbers

**Summary for the author.** Two kinds of number exist. The OCP specification fixes the interface: 16 UCIe channels per stack, three speed grades (0.384, 1.536 and 3.072 TB/s), 512 GiB per 16-high stack, 4 KiB pages, 24 h power-on retention. Everything that depends on the NAND itself (read latency, write bandwidth, endurance, power, cell type, price) is "product specific" and unpublished; the only figures are Sandisk marketing numbers, a Google "ballpark" table, and simulator assumptions in academic papers. No HBF silicon measurement was located. The single most useful derived figure is bandwidth divided by capacity: an HBM4 stack can be read end to end about 55 to 90 times a second, an HBF stack about 1.4 to 5.6 times a second.

## 4. Measured results and independent analysis

**Summary for the author.** As of 2026-10-17 no HBF silicon measurement has been published that this search located. Sandisk announced tape-out of its first HBF memory die on 2026-08-13 and described its own performance numbers as simulations. Everything below is therefore one of three things: vendor simulation, academic simulation (arXiv, not yet peer-reviewed), or third-party modelling reported by trade press. The academic work converges on one result: HBF helps when it holds read-mostly data that would otherwise force more GPUs or more off-package traffic (weights, MoE experts, sparse long-context KV, multi-model residency), and it hurts when used as a drop-in write-heavy KV-cache pool.

## 5. Which inference data it suits

**Summary for the author.** The fit test is reads per write, and how much of an object each decode step touches. Every source that states a placement puts frozen weights (MoE expert pools above all), shared or precomputed context, multi-model residency and large read-mostly corpora on HBF, and keeps per-token transient KV cache and activations in HBM or SRAM. Two flash behaviours mean "read-only" data still costs writes. The specification's retention refresh (every 24-48 h) rewrites resident data 0.5 to 1 times a day [derived]. The per-block read counter forces a block rewrite after a product-specific number of reads, and in FLINT's simulation of a weight block that decode reads every token, read disturb sets device lifetime: 29 days at 10^5 P/E cycles, 8 years at a projected 10^7. Both limits depend on device numbers no vendor has published. KV cache on HBF is the contested case: one paper finds it feasible for SLC flash and a compact (MLA) KV format, another finds a TLC tier under production traces wears out at 0.56x the life of the SSD pool it replaced.

## 6. What it changes in the memory hierarchy

**Summary for the author.** HBF adds a tier the hierarchy never had: terabytes of capacity on the accelerator package, at 0.4 to 3.1 TB/s per stack against 2.8 to 3.3 TB/s for a shipping HBM4 stack, but swept end to end only 1.4 to 5.6 times a second against 57 to 92 for HBM4 (section 3). It sits between HBM and host memory in both capacity and bandwidth, yet it attaches to the accelerator and not to the CPU, so its closest competitor for the same data is the HBM beside it, not host DRAM, CXL or NVMe. It shares HBM's package sites, so on a fixed package each HBF stack buys about 476 GB of capacity with about 1.2 to 1.7 TB/s of bandwidth [derived]. It is a software-managed, non-coherent memory: the serving engine places data, orders writes, schedules refresh and reloads from storage after power loss. It does not replace the SSD, which the specification still recommends for persistence.

### Read-disturb sensitivity (worst case: a stack read flat out)

[derived] Inputs: Sandisk Gen 1, 1.6 TB/s over 512 GB, 3.125 sweeps per second [S2-1]; 1,024 pages per block (FLINT's assumption [S1-11]); the read counter is per block [S1-1] s4.4. Every block then collects 3,125 x 1.024 = 3,200 page reads a second, and is due for refresh after X / 3,200 seconds. Lifetime from read-disturb refresh alone = rated P/E x refresh interval. Real workloads read less than flat out, so these are lower bounds for the stated inputs; retention refresh (once per 24-48 h) adds 0.5 to 1 P/E per day on top.

| Read-disturb threshold X | Refresh interval per block | Write rate to keep up (whole stack) | Lifetime at 10^5 P/E | at 10^6 P/E | at 10^7 P/E |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10^5 reads | 31 s | 16.4 GB/s | 36 days | 0.99 years | 9.9 years |
| 10^6 reads | 312 s | 1.6 GB/s | 0.99 years | 9.9 years | 99 years |

The P/E columns follow FLINT's sweep (10^5 "commodity-SLC floor" to a "projected" 10^7); the X rows follow the 10^5 to 10^6 range FLINT cites for SLC. HBF's actual values for all three inputs are unpublished.

## Author guidance

**Strongest mental model, in one line.** An HBF stack is a 512 GB read-mostly shelf on the accelerator package that can be read end to end about 3 times a second (an HBM4 stack: about 80), so it pays off for data that is large, rarely rewritten, and only partly touched per token.

**Facts a reader can act on.**
1. **Size placement by sweeps per second, not by bandwidth.** Bandwidth divided by capacity is 3.1 per second for a Sandisk Gen 1 stack (1.6 TB/s over 512 GB, VENDOR CLAIM [S2-1]) and 78 to 92 for shipping HBM4 (VENDOR CLAIM + [derived], M:T2-9, M:T2-13). A decode step cannot finish faster than the bytes it reads divided by the bandwidth: a dense 405B model at FP8 on eight Gen 1 stacks caps at about 32 tokens a second per sequence at batch 1 [derived] (row 6.13). Put MoE expert pools there, where each token reads a few percent of the weights (DeepSeek-V3: 37B of 671B, M:T2-29).
2. **Treat HBF as a cache that is reloaded from storage.** The specification guarantees 24 h of power-on retention at 85 C, makes the host refresh data every 24-48 h, does not guarantee retention without power and recommends SSDs for persistence (SPEC [S1-1] s9, s11.5). Keep the SSD load path and budget its reload time.
3. **Keep per-token KV cache and activations off it.** Google calls HBF "infeasible for KV Cache data updated for every query or generation token" [S1-6]; a simulated drop-in HBF KV pool wore out at 0.56x the life of the SSD pool it replaced (SIMULATED [S1-7]). The specification puts activations in the optional base-die SRAM and recommends separate channel groups for weights and KV (SPEC [S1-1] s11.3, s13.3.3).
4. **Ask for three datasheet numbers before committing: read latency, rated P/E (MAXPEC) and the read-disturb threshold.** None is published (UNKNOWN). For a stack read flat out, the read-disturb threshold and P/E rating alone move device life from 36 days to 99 years [derived] (section 8).
5. **There is nothing to buy in 2026.** As of 2026-10-16 there is a v0.7.0 specification and one taped-out die; Sandisk targets samples of inference devices with HBF in 2027 (ROADMAP [S2-10]); no accelerator vendor has announced an HBF product (absence finding); 2028 production is a brokerage estimate (Tier 3 [S3-5]).

**Suggested h2 headings, each with its payoff.**
1. **"A NAND stack wired like HBM"**: how 16 NAND dies on a controller base die deliver 0.4 to 3 TB/s and 512 GiB per stack, and which jobs (write order, wear levelling, refresh) move from the drive to your software.
2. **"Sweeps per second decides what belongs on it"**: one division (bandwidth over capacity) separates HBF at about 3 a second from HBM4 at about 80, and predicts which data fits.
3. **"What to put on it and what to keep in HBM"**: MoE expert pools, resident models and shared context go on HBF; per-token KV cache and activations stay in HBM or SRAM.
4. **"Reading wears flash too"**: why retention and read-disturb refresh turn reads into writes, and the three datasheet numbers to ask a vendor for.
5. **"Each HBF site trades HBM bandwidth for capacity"**: swapping one HBM4 stack for one HBF stack adds about 476 GB and gives up 1.2 to 1.7 TB/s, and where that leaves host DRAM, CXL and NVMe.
6. **"What exists today and the dates to watch"**: a published interface, a taped-out die, device samples targeted for 2027, and the next vendor update on 2026-10-29. This ends the section on the reader's next action.
